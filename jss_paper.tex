\begin{abstract}
Real-time requirements in Metric Temporal Logic are checked by translating their negation into a timed B\"uchi automaton (TBA) that UPPAAL or TChecker composes with the system. We present a chain from MTL requirements to timed automata whose optimization and export stages are verified in Rocq. The passes preserve the timed-word language of every TBA over the formula-indexed clock interface. Two verified policies eliminate disjunctive location invariants, and the all-copy policy yields automata that TChecker's liveness algorithms accept. An end-to-end theorem composes the passes with a verified MTL translation. The shipped tool runs Spot, so its guarantee holds under a stated hypothesis on Spot's output and when an initial-value check succeeds, which failed on 4 of 100 random formulas; Spot, its readers and the XML printer remain trusted. Translation validation with an independent translator found no discrepancy on 123 of 131 runs; the other eight were too large for it. Against the translator CASAAL, our automata have the same size on the negated requirements except on three patterns, where they are smaller, and over 18 products TChecker stores 204,564 states on ours and 204,819 on CASAAL's converted to location acceptance. The optimizer does not change what TChecker explores, but without it the TChecker-readable export of five bounded responses does not finish in 300~s on our machine. Generation is slower than CASAAL's on deeply nested requirements, where Spot dominates. Of 22 seeded defects in the extracted code, 18 change the language and a random-word test misses five; Rocq rejects all 22 transcribed mutants, which shows the sensitivity of the proofs to edits and not defect detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Safety and liveness requirements of real-time systems are commonly written in Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) and checked by translating the negated requirement into a timed B\"uchi automaton (TBA), which a timed model checker such as UPPAAL~\cite{[BGD04]} or TChecker~\cite{tchecker} composes with the system model. The size and shape of that automaton matter in practice. Clocks multiply the symbolic state space the checker has to explore; repeated bounds and resets that never influence a later guard enlarge the automaton without changing its language; and UPPAAL accepts only conjunctive guards and conjunctive upper-bound invariants, whereas merging transitions naturally produces disjunctions.

Optimizing a TBA is risky. A pass that is wrong in a rare case silently changes the set of timed words the property accepts, and a model checker then reports a verdict about a different requirement. The passes also interact: propagating a lower bound can make a transition contradictory. Splitting a disjunctive invariant into copies by a single entry choice is safe only if the entry condition picks a copy whose invariant admits no less time than the others; the all-copy policy of Section~\ref{sec:split} avoids that condition by entering every copy. The seeded-defect study of Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} shows that a random-word test can miss defects of this kind.

This paper presents a post-processing pipeline for TBAs whose every pass is mechanically verified in Rocq, together with its use in a translator from MTL. The \emph{optimization} stage synthesizes upper-bound location invariants, propagates them into guards and entry bounds, removes contradictory transitions and dead resets, merges clocks that are always reset together, normalizes guards, and merges equivalent transitions and locations. The \emph{export} stage merges transitions by disjoining their guards, eliminates disjunctive invariants by creating copies with conjunctive invariants and clock-difference entry conditions, and normalizes the result so that guards and invariants are conjunctive. We prove that each pass preserves the timed-word language of every input TBA, that the optimizer does so for every number of rounds, and that the output of export has the conjunctive shape UPPAAL needs.

The passes act on TBAs, whatever their origin. To obtain an end-to-end guarantee for MTL requirements we compose them with a translation whose correctness has been established separately in Rocq~\cite{faresmtl}: a verified translation of MTL$_{0,\infty}$ into a TBA, factored through clocked Linear Temporal Logic and proved correct for a verified propositional B\"uchi backend. Section~\ref{sec:composition} states the resulting theorem, which has no hypothesis on the backend when the verified backend is used; the tool runs Spot, whose conformance to the backend contract is a hypothesis (Section~\ref{sec:composition}). This paper does not repeat the translation proofs; Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the definitions the passes are stated over, so the article can be read on its own.

The passes are designed to work together and are proved as a composition: normalizing guards makes dead resets visible, removing dead resets makes more clocks synchronous, and merging them lets transitions and locations merge, so that the verified optimizer is iterated and its theorem holds for every number of rounds. The result is a \emph{modular, mechanized chain}: every stage except the call to Spot has a Rocq theorem stated against an interface, and the propositional backend is a parameter (Section~\ref{sec:modular}).

The verified passes are extracted to OCaml and run inside the translator \texttt{mtl2tba}, which calls Spot~\cite{spot} for the propositional automaton and writes UPPAAL XML. We ask four questions of the pipeline.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,labelindent=0pt]
  \item[RQ1] How does the whole translation compare with an independent translator, CASAAL~\cite{casaal_tool}, in the size of the automaton, in generation time and in the states a timed model checker stores?
  \item[RQ2] What does the export cost, which export policy can the checkers read, and where does optimization fail to help?
  \item[RQ3] Which Spot setting should a user choose, and what does the call to Spot cost on deeply nested requirements?
  \item[RQ4] What does verification add: which plausible defects does testing miss that the proofs reject?
\end{description}
The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item a modular chain from MTL to the exported timed automaton whose stages are stated against interfaces, so that the propositional backend (Spot, or any translator that meets the contract) can be replaced without changing the optimization and export theorems; Spot, the readers of its output, the XML printer and UPPAAL lie outside the proofs;
  \item a Rocq development of language-preserving TBA transformations (invariant synthesis and propagation, clock and location simplification, transition merging, and disjunctive-invariant elimination), with preservation theorems for every TBA of the formula-indexed clock interface and every number of optimization rounds;
  \item two verified policies for eliminating disjunctive location invariants by location copies, the maximal-delay policy (which may introduce clock-difference constraints) and the all-copy policy (which enters every copy and introduces none), each with a language-preservation proof; the all-copy export is the one that TChecker's liveness algorithms accept (Sections~\ref{sec:split} and~\ref{sec:export-policies});
  \item a verified export whose guards and invariants are conjunctive, and an end-to-end theorem for MTL requirements that composes it with a verified translation, without hypotheses on the propositional backend when the verified backend is used;
  \item verified rewritings of the pass implementations that make the extracted code practical on automata of thousands of transitions, and an automatic default for Spot's level; and
  \item an evaluation of the whole translation against CASAAL in output size, generation time, TChecker stored states and UPPAAL verdicts; a differential test of both tools against an independent MTL evaluator (6{,}160{,}000 word checks, no disagreement); a seeded-defect study (22 bugs injected in the extracted code, of which a random-word test misses five of the 18 that change the language, while the corresponding mistakes are rejected by the Rocq development); and the negative results: no measurable model-checking gain from optimization, a large gap on one constructed workload that is not an effect of the verified passes, and slower generation than CASAAL on deeply nested requirements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Timed Automata and the Preservation Contract}
\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Timed words and TBAs}
A timed word is an infinite sequence $w=(a_i,t_i)_{i\in\Nat}$, where $a_i$ is an event from a finite alphabet $\Sigma$, timestamps satisfy $t_i\ge0$ and are nondecreasing, and time diverges: $\lim_{i\to\infty}t_i=+\infty$. For each position $i$, let $\delta_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$ be the time from event $i$ to the next event. A clock valuation maps a finite clock set $X$ to $\Real_{\ge0}$. Guards of a TBA are finite conjunctions of event literals and unary clock comparisons $x\bowtie c$ for $\bowtie\in\{<,\le,=,\ge,>\}$; transitions also carry a set of clocks to reset. The exported automaton (a \emph{DTA} below) is a TBA whose guards may in addition contain diagonal comparisons $x-y\bowtie c$ (Section~\ref{sec:split}), and whose locations carry conjunctive upper-bound invariants. In the \emph{formula-indexed clock interface} used by the Rocq development, the clock set is the set of timed subformulas of a fixed formula, which serve only as clock names; an automaton with $k$ clocks is represented by choosing a formula with at least $k$ timed subformulas.

A TBA is a tuple $\mathcal A=(L,\ell_0,X,E,\mathit{Acc})$, where $L$ is a finite location set, $\ell_0$ is the initial location, $X$ is the clock set, $E$ is a finite set of transitions $(\ell,\alpha,g,Z,\ell')$, $\alpha$ is an event condition, $g$ is a clock guard, $Z\subseteq X$ is a reset set, and $\mathit{Acc}\subseteq L$ is the set of B\"uchi-accepting locations. The starting automata in the verified optimization are represented without location invariants; synthesis adds them as a separate annotation. Exported invariants are conjunctions of upper bounds.

The Rocq development interprets transitions over an extended timed word that records clock values and reset decisions. Here $v_i$ is the clock valuation at event $i$, and $Z_i$ records the clocks reset by the transition on that event. Since a reset occurs at $t_i$, the next-event valuation includes the elapsed interval $\delta_i$:
\[
v_{i+1}(x)=
\begin{cases}
\delta_i,&x\in Z_i,\\
v_i(x)+\delta_i,&x\notin Z_i.
\end{cases}
\]
A run chooses one enabled transition per position and visits accepting locations infinitely often. The accepted language existentially quantifies over clock-consistent extensions of the base timed word, including the initial clock values. This is the acceptance notion of the preservation theorems; conventional initialization at zero is a separate deployment concern, bridged by the verified check \path{init_free} (Section~\ref{sec:toolchain}), which succeeds when no clock can be read before it is reset from the initial location.

MTL$_{0,\infty}$ is metric temporal logic with the pointwise semantics over timed words, whose timed operators carry intervals with lower bound $0$ or upper bound $\infty$; $\mathsf{msat}(w,i,f)$ states that $w$ satisfies $f$ at position $i$, and the requirement is satisfied by $w$ when $\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)$ holds. The definitions are those of~\cite{faresmtl}.

For an annotated automaton, an invariant must hold throughout the delay spent at its location. Since synthesized invariants are conjunctions of non-strict upper bounds and clocks are nondecreasing during a delay, holding at the event at the end of the delay is equivalent to holding throughout that stay. The proof formalizes this trace-model equivalence and uses it to show that the annotations and strengthening operations preserve timed words.

\subsection{What export must preserve}
\label{sec:export-contract}
The export must retain the same transition alphabet, B\"uchi marking, clock meaning, and timed-word language. It may change the number of locations, transitions, and clocks, and may introduce difference constraints in transition guards to choose among invariant copies. Such choices must not create or delete an accepted timed word. The target representation has conjunctive guards and conjunctive location invariants; a later symbolic representation groups transitions with identical source, target, and reset sets under Boolean labels.

The preservation theorems hold for every TBA represented by the formula-indexed clock interface, independently of whether the automaton was generated by the MTL translator. Their proof does not require properties of the MTL translation or Spot. An upstream correctness theorem can be composed with these results, but Spot's correctness and the soundness of the front end are outside the pass theorems.

\section{Verified Optimization}
\label{sec:post}

Propagating them to incoming guards can make lower and upper constraints jointly inconsistent; forward propagation supplies complementary lower-bound information. The pipeline then removes transitions, resets, clocks, and duplicate structure that no longer affect the language.

The proofs of the passes are mechanized in Rocq (Figure~\ref{fig:main-inv-unified} shows the invariant computation); the development of this paper (invariants, optimization, export and symbolic grouping) is 6,105 lines with 251 theorems and lemmas, on top of the translation of~\cite{faresmtl}. Each pass is a total function on TBAs with a preservation theorem listed in Table~\ref{tab:post-passes}; the reasoning is the same for all: a run of the output is mapped to a run of the input (or conversely) location by location, and the changed component (a guard, an invariant, a reset set, a clock or a location) is shown to be implied or equivalent at every event. In brief:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
\item \emph{Invariant synthesis} adds, at each location, the upper bound that the outgoing guards require; \emph{backward propagation} strengthens incoming guards with the target invariant (reset clocks become constants); \emph{forward propagation} adds the lower bounds implied by the entry bounds.
\item \emph{Contradictory transitions} (unsatisfiable guards or labels) and \emph{unreachable outgoing transitions} are removed; \emph{dead resets} (resets on edges entering a location from which no test of the clock is reachable, even through resets) are removed and \emph{synchronously reset clocks} are merged.
\item \emph{Normalization} drops tautological and subsumed atoms; \emph{transition merging} fuses transitions only by subsumption and resolution, that is, when the union of two guards is again a conjunction (disjunctive unions are left to export); \emph{location merging} fuses locations that have the same outgoing behaviour.
\end{itemize}
The definitions, the invariant semantics on the trace model and the proofs are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:passes}.

\subsection{Iterated optimization}
\label{sec:main-pipeline}
In right-to-left function-composition order, one optimization round is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{optimize\_step}={}&\mathsf{merge\_states}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{merge\_trans}\circ\mathsf{normalize}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{merge\_clocks}\circ\mathsf{dead\_resets}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{normalize}\circ\mathsf{unreachable}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{unsat\_labels}\circ\mathsf{contradictory}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{forward}\circ\mathsf{propagate}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The order is deliberate. Forward propagation can add $x\ge0$ after a reset;
the first normalization removes this tautology before dead-reset analysis,
which would otherwise treat the clock as live. Each operation may enable
later simplifications, so the driver repeats the round until the TBA is
structurally unchanged or 50 rounds have run. The theorem is independent of
this stopping rule and holds for every $n$.

\begin{theorem}[Optimization]
\label{thm:optimization}
For every TBA $\mathcal A$ and every $n\in\Nat$, $\optimize(n,\mathcal A)$ accepts exactly the timed words accepted by $\mathcal A$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every constituent operation preserves acceptance, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:post-passes}. Their composition preserves acceptance, and induction on $n$ proves the statement for every number of rounds. The mechanized result is \path{optimize_accepts}.
\end{proof}

The extracted function also returns synthesized upper-bound invariants and entry bounds. The tool compares successive automata structurally and stops when they are equal or after 50 rounds. The theorem holds independently of whether iteration reaches a fixed point or uses all 50 rounds. Among the 29 completed benchmark inputs, the largest observed number of changed rounds is six (R6); this is a measured property of these inputs, not a termination assumption in the proof. The practical 50-round cap is a driver policy, not a premise of \path{optimize_accepts}.

\section{Mechanically Verified Elimination of Disjunctive Invariants}
\label{sec:split}
Disjunctive invariants do not arise in the optimization of Section~\ref{sec:post}, whose synthesized invariants are conjunctions of upper bounds. They arise in export, where transitions that agree on source, target, label and resets are merged by disjoining their guards, so that the upper bounds that the outgoing guards of a location impose form a disjunction of conjunctions. This section eliminates such disjunctions.

\subsection{A worked example: selecting a longest admissible delay}
\label{subsec:disjunctive-worked-example}
Suppose the invariant synthesized at a location $q$ is
$I_x\lor I_y$, where $I_x\equiv x\le3$ and $I_y\equiv y\le4$. From the
entry valuation $(x,y)=(0,0)$, the original disjunction allows a stay of up
to four time units: $I_y$ remains true after $I_x$ has expired. A single
copy carrying only $I_x$ would stop the run after three units and could lose
a timed word whose next event occurs at time four. The copies therefore
cannot be selected by an arbitrary disjunct.

The split creates $q_x$ with invariant $x\le3$ and $q_y$ with invariant
$y\le4$. A copy is enabled on entry only when it admits a delay at least as
long as every other copy. With the difference-constraint convention
$u-v\le c$, the entry predicates are
\[
  G_x \equiv x\le3\ \land\ x-y\le-1,
  \qquad
  G_y \equiv y\le4\ \land\ y-x\le1.
\]
At $(0,0)$, $G_y$ holds and $G_x$ does not, so the four-unit branch is
selected. At the tie valuation $(0,1)$, both predicates hold and both
copies admit the same maximal delay. Comparing residual times
$3-x$ and $4-y$ requires the clock-difference constraints; separate unary
guards cannot express their ordering.

The reset-adjustment step is visible when an incoming edge resets $x$ but
leaves $y$ unchanged. Write $y^{-}$ for the value of $y$ just before the
edge; immediately after the edge, $x=0$ and $y=y^{-}$. Substituting this
reset into the two entry predicates gives
\[
 G_x[x:=0]\equiv y\ge1,
 \qquad
 G_y[x:=0]\equiv y\le1.
\]
Thus $y^{-}>1$ admits only the $x$-copy, $y^{-}<1$ only the $y$-copy, and the
tie $y^{-}=1$ admits both. The comparison of two clocks has become a unary comparison
because one clock was reset. If the edge resets both clocks, a difference
constraint becomes a constant truth test; if it resets neither, the diagonal
comparison remains. The case in which only $y$ is reset is symmetric to the first; together these are the four reset cases handled by the
verified guard-substitution function.

\subsection{General construction and the two split policies}
\label{subsec:split-construction}
For a disjunction $I_1\lor\cdots\lor I_r$ of upper-bound conjunctions, the construction creates one copy of the location per irredundant conjunction $U_k$, carrying $U_k$ as its invariant. The \emph{maximal-delay} policy admits a copy on entry only when its largest admissible delay is at least that of every other copy; this needs clock-difference (diagonal) entry guards, which are adjusted by the verified guard substitution for the reset cases of the example above. The \emph{all-copy} policy lets every copy be entered and relies on the covering property proved inside the construction (\path{disj_inv_cover}); it is nondeterministic and introduces no diagonal constraint. Both preserve the timed-word language (\path{split_accepts}, \path{split_all_accepts}); the full definitions, the proofs of both and a measured comparison on synthetic automata are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-subsec:split-detail}. The measured trade-off is not uniform: on a two-clock example all-copy uses four more edges but fewer guard atoms and no diagonal atoms, on a six-branch family both give 68 edges and all-copy has fewer atoms; Section~\ref{sec:export-policies} compares the two policies on the benchmark. The default remains maximal delay; all-copy is separately verified and used whenever TChecker's liveness algorithms must read the automaton.


\section{Verified UPPAAL-Oriented Export}
\label{sec:export}
\subsection{Generated continuation example}
Supplement Figure~\ref{S-fig:R2-generated} follows an actual current tool-generated automaton from the reset-completion boundary through optimization and export. It does not repeat the upstream MTL translation. In this instance, two optimization rounds remove nine transitions; export adds three explicit transitions while producing the required conjunctive internal shape.

The export continues by splitting disjunctive guards into conjunctive transitions, normalizing, removing copies that are never entered, and merging the initial location when an equivalent copy exists. A final symbolic pass groups transitions with the same source, target, and resets and stores their labels as Boolean conditions for drawings.

\subsection{Whole-export preservation and output shape}
\begin{theorem}[Export preservation]
\label{thm:export}
For every invariant-free TBA provided to the verified export interface, the explicit export preserves its timed-word language. The resulting automaton has conjunctive transition guards and conjunctive location invariants.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The explicit construction composes verified transition merging, invariant splitting, guard splitting, normalization, removal of copies never entered, and initial-location merging; its timed-word preservation result is \path{export_accepts}. The output shape is established by \path{export_guards_conjunctive} and \path{export_invariants_conjunctive}. A separate symbolic grouping pass preserves timed-word acceptance by \path{symbolic_accepts} (with the extended-word result \path{symbolic_ext_accepts}).
\end{proof}
For reuse with another producer or checker, the theorem's output contract is
summarized in Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:export-contract}: the language is preserved, guards and invariants are conjunctive, and a third-party parser or conversion needs its own correspondence proof.

The theorem holds for every TBA of the formula-indexed clock interface: it requires no MTL provenance,
determinism, or particular upstream producer.

The saved generated files for the R2 input, optimized automaton, and export
are provided in \path{evaluation/generated_examples/R2/}. Their run
metadata are recorded alongside the files. This example shows how the optimizer and the export interact, while the separate synthetic ablation
in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} isolates pass effects on direct TBA records.


\section{End-to-End Guarantee for MTL Requirements}
\label{sec:composition}
The passes of Sections~\ref{sec:post}--\ref{sec:export} are stated over arbitrary TBAs. For MTL requirements they are used behind a translation $\mathsf{compile\_with}(A)$ that turns a propositional B\"uchi automaton $A$ for the clocked-LTL encoding $\T(f)$ of an MTL$_{0,\infty}$ formula $f$ into a TBA. Its correctness, in the form we need, is the result of~\cite{faresmtl}: if $A$ accepts exactly the action-consistent propositional words that satisfy $\T(f)$ (the \emph{backend contract}) and $f$ is well formed (nonnegative non-strict upper bounds, strictly positive bounds for the other timed operators), then
\[
\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)\ \leftrightarrow\ \mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(\mathsf{compile\_with}(A),w)
\]
for every timed word $w$ with nondecreasing timestamps (the words of Section~\ref{sec:model} are a subclass). The same work provides a propositional automaton $\mathsf{backend}(f,\T(f))$ for which the backend contract is proved in Rocq.

Our development imports these definitions and results, and instantiates the generic composition with that backend:
\begin{theorem}[End-to-end correctness with the verified backend]
\label{thm:composition}
For every $n\in\Nat$, every well-formed MTL$_{0,\infty}$ formula $f$ and every timed word $w$,
\[
  \begin{multlined}
  \mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)\ \leftrightarrow\\
  \mathsf{DTA\_accepts}\bigl(\export(\optimize(n,\mathsf{compile\_with}(\mathsf{backend}(f,\T(f))))),\,w\bigr).
  \end{multlined}
\]
The same holds with the all-copy exporter of Section~\ref{sec:split} in place of $\export$. Here the invariant-free TBA component of the result of $\optimize$ is the input of $\export$; the invariants and entry bounds that the extracted function also returns are not passed to it.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $C=\mathsf{compile\_with}(\mathsf{backend}(f,\T(f)))$, $O_n=\optimize(n,C)$ and $D_n=\export(O_n)$. The translation result gives $\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(C,w)$, the backend contract being discharged by the verified backend. Preservation by optimization (Theorem~\ref{thm:optimization}) gives $\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(C,w)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(O_n,w)$, and preservation by export (Theorem~\ref{thm:export}) gives $\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(O_n,w)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{DTA\_accepts}(D_n,w)$. The Rocq statements are \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_verified} and \path{MTL_to_exported_all_correct_verified}; \texttt{Print Assumptions} lists only the standard axioms of the Rocq library (classical logic, functional extensionality, constructive definite description, and the real-number axiom of the standard library).
\end{proof}
The general form \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_with} replaces the verified backend by any automaton $A$ that satisfies the backend contract. The verified backend does no simplification and is a proof witness only; it is not the one used by the extracted tool.

The theorem that the shipped tool instantiates is \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_recur_weak_with}. Its statement is: for every $n$, every well-formed $f$, every propositional automaton $A$ for the rewritten formula $\mathsf{recur}(f)$ that accepts the action-consistent words satisfying the weakened clocked encoding of $\mathsf{recur}(f)$, and every timed word $w$, if \path{init_free} succeeds on $\export(\optimize(n,\mathsf{compile\_with}(A)))$ then $\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)$ holds iff that automaton accepts $w$ under the zero-initialized reading. Spot supplies $A$; whether its output satisfies the hypothesis on $A$ is not proved, and is tested only through the differential checks of Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds}. After a run, a user is entitled to believe that the exported automaton accepts the requested language provided Spot's automaton met the backend contract, \path{init_free} succeeded, and the trusted components listed in Section~\ref{sec:toolchain} behaved correctly.

No convergence premise is imposed on $n$: preservation is proved by induction for every finite number of rounds, so the driver's stop after a structural fixed point or after 50 rounds is a policy that does not enter the theorem. The conclusion concerns the internal exported record and the existential initial-clock semantics of Section~\ref{sec:model}. A second theorem, \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_with}, gives the zero-initialized reading whenever a check named \path{init_free} succeeds on the exported automaton. Neither theorem verifies the HOA/LBTT importer, the XML printer or parser, or UPPAAL itself (Section~\ref{sec:toolchain}).

\subsection{A modular chain: what can be replaced}
\label{sec:modular}
Theorem~\ref{thm:composition} is a composition of stages, and each is
stated against an interface, not against the stage before it.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Clocked-LTL translation} $\T$: from an MTL$_{0,\infty}$ formula to a
    formula over event, clock and reset atoms (proved in~\cite{faresmtl}).
  \item \emph{Propositional backend}: any B\"uchi automaton $A$ that accepts the
    action-consistent propositional words satisfying $\T(f)$, the backend
    contract of Section~\ref{sec:composition}. The theorem
    \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_with} takes the contract as a hypothesis;
    \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_verified} discharges it with the verified
    backend, which is a proof witness and slow.
  \item \emph{Reset completion} \path{compile_with}: from $A$ to a TBA of the
    formula-indexed clock interface (proved in~\cite{faresmtl}).
  \item \emph{Optimization} and \emph{export}: language-preserving passes over
    every TBA of that interface (this paper).
\end{enumerate}
Two consequences follow, and the development already uses the first.

First, the backend is a parameter. Any LTL-to-B\"uchi translator whose output
satisfies the contract can replace Spot without touching a proof: the tool
reads the output of any translator that accepts Spot's command line
(\texttt{-spot}) or whose output is converted to HOA or LBTT, and the
guarantee of the chain is then that of the contract the user, or a proof,
establishes for that translator. In this paper we changed Spot's level, its options and the format in which we read its output, and we replaced the list scans of the passes by tables with equality lemmas (Section~\ref{sec:speedups}), each time with no change to the statement of any theorem. The verified
backend of the development shows the other end of the range: a translator
with a proof of the contract and a poor running time.

Second, the stages in front of the optimizer can be replaced together. A construction that produces a TBA directly from the MTL formula, as CASAAL does with the construction of Li et al.~\cite{li_spin7}, would be composed with the same optimization and export theorems; the first equivalence of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:composition} would be replaced by the correctness theorem of the new construction. We have not written such a construction; it is the replacement that would remove the dependency on Spot, whose output is outside the proofs.

This design has a price, which Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures: on deeply nested requirements the generic LTL translator is the slow part of the chain, at 60 to 90\% of the time on seven levels (Section~\ref{sec:casaal-time}), while the verified stages are not. A purpose-built front end might reduce that time; this is an expectation based on one profile, not a measurement.

\section{Tool Architecture and Use in UPPAAL}
\label{sec:toolchain}
\subsection{Implementation and proof boundary}
The OCaml module \texttt{optim.ml} is extracted from Rocq and contains the
verified optimization and export functions. The integrated executable also
contains handwritten input, iteration, and output code. These adapters and
the XML/DOT printers are outside the transformation proofs. A converter from
an external automaton format must establish its own correspondence with the
Rocq TBA type. A clean Rocq 9.0.1 build, independent \texttt{coqchk} run,
and OCaml 5.4.0 rebuild are recorded in the accompanying artifact audit.
The UPPAAL 5.0.0 queries agree with the recorded verdicts.

The project-wide axiom \path{LTL_TO_BUCHI_CORRECT} states the assumed correctness of the upstream LTL-to-B\"uchi backend and is used only by convenience theorems. The theorems \path{optimize_accepts}, \path{split_accepts}, \path{export_accepts} and \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_with} do not use it: \texttt{Print Assumptions} lists only standard classical Rocq library principles (including \path{ClassicalDedekindReals.sig_forall_dec}), and the artifact records the results.

In the integrated workflow the translation of~\cite{faresmtl} produces the TBA at the optimizer boundary; this paper concerns only the subsequent optimizer and exporter. The driver iterates optimization until structural stability or a 50-round cap, a policy that does not enter the theorems, and then applies export.

\subsection{Choosing the Spot level: automatic mode and manual override}
\label{sec:spot-level}
The propositional automaton is produced by \texttt{ltl2tgba -B --<level> [-x <options>] --hoa} and read back by the tool (the reader is outside the proofs). The theorems hold for every automaton that meets the backend contract, so level and options affect running time a great deal, the size of the result little, and correctness not at all. No level is best everywhere: level \texttt{low} is fast on conjunctions of bounded responses and slow on deeply nested bounded eventualities, and level \texttt{any} the reverse; Spot's simulation-based reduction removed nothing on our inputs and takes more than half of its time at level \texttt{any}, so we switch it off (\texttt{-x ba-simul=0}). The default is therefore an \emph{automatic mode} (\texttt{-spot-level auto}): it runs Spot at level \texttt{low} and, if Spot has not finished after a budget of $b$ seconds (\texttt{-spot-budget}, default 5), stops it and runs level \texttt{any} with \texttt{ba-simul=0} on the formula simplified by the proved simplification \path{ltl_simp_correct}. Both steps are instances of the verified translation and only Spot remains trusted. The fallback costs at most $b$ seconds, and the choice depends on a wall-clock measurement, so the level, and with it the run time, can differ between runs and machines; the mode can be overridden with \texttt{-spot-level}, \texttt{-simp} and \texttt{-spot-x}, and benchmarking should fix the level. The measurements behind these statements are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:spot-level-detail}; Section~\ref{sec:casaal-time} reports automatic and manual times side by side.

\subsection{Initialization interface}
The preservation theorems use existential initial clock values, whereas UPPAAL initializes clocks to zero. The proved \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_with} theorem gives the zero-initialized result for the formula-indexed workflow when the verified \path{init_free} check succeeds. All 29 completed benchmark outputs pass that check (the two timed-out configurations produce no output), and the same check applies to an external automaton.

\subsection{Using exported automata in UPPAAL}
The language-preservation theorem concerns infinite timed words with one event at each position, nondecreasing timestamps, divergent time, and the development's clock initialization semantics. A UPPAAL composition must preserve the assumptions needed to interpret its traces in that model:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
  \item \emph{Events and time.} The system must synchronize each observable event with the property automaton, prevent zero-delay consecutive events (for example, reset a system clock $z$ on each emission and require $z>0$ on the next), and admit time-divergent executions; reachability alone does not rule out a Zeno continuation.
  \item \emph{Initial clocks.} UPPAAL initializes clocks to zero, so the zero-initialized conclusion requires the \path{init_free} check to succeed on the exported automaton.
  \item \emph{Acceptance.} UPPAAL's reachability interface does not check B\"uchi acceptance. For safety, use a violation automaton with an accepting sink that is reached when a violation occurs and loops on every event; liveness requires a B\"uchi emptiness checker restricted to time-divergent runs, for which the artifact uses TChecker.
  \item \emph{Time constraints in products.} Location invariants can block time in a product and thereby remove system behaviors, so an exported property automaton is not a passive observer unless its effect is separately justified; check deadlock or timelock conditions on the system.
  \item \emph{Numbers.} The tool accepts integer bounds in $[0,2^{30}]$, while the Rocq theorems are over real-valued clocks; the extracted arithmetic and the UPPAAL printer are covered only on that restricted domain.
\end{enumerate}

The generated XML has a \texttt{Property} template for the automaton and an \texttt{Env} template for standalone exploration; in a system check the system replaces \texttt{Env} and synchronizes its observable events with \texttt{Property}, and the event \texttt{other} denotes any event outside the alphabet of the formula. Accepting locations are named \texttt{A}$n$, which does not make UPPAAL check B\"uchi acceptance. For a safety requirement, under (C1)--(C3) the query \texttt{E<> P.A} on a violation automaton composed with such a system detects a violating behavior; this query-level argument is not proved by the Rocq theorems.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\emph{Internal validity.} The theorems concern the Rocq model of automata; the extracted OCaml functions are the verified ones, but the surrounding tool is not. The trusted base is: Spot and the reader of its output; the realization of reals by floating point and the extraction mechanism; the parser, the iteration driver, the pretty printers and the UPPAAL XML writer; the importer used for CASAAL's automata; Graphviz; the OCaml and Rocq toolchains; UPPAAL and TChecker; the check \path{init_free}; and the project axiom \path{LTL_TO_BUCHI_CORRECT}, which only convenience theorems use. We reduce this risk with a second printer, a reader check (\texttt{-check}) and independent exploration of the case-study models, but these are tests, not proofs.

\emph{Construct validity.} CASAAL implements the semantics of Li et al.\ with integer bounds and mutually exclusive events, and it reports transition-based acceptance. We compare only configurations whose CASAAL encoding is exact in that setting and convert its acceptance to locations; the importer and the accepting-state convention are our choices, and a different encoding could give different counts. Stored states are a property of the pair (automaton, checker), and generation times compare a Windows executable with our tool under WSL~2, so they are not precise ratios.

\emph{External validity.} The requirement patterns are a small corpus drawn from the literature and from parameterized families, and the system models are small. Results do not predict behavior on industrial specifications, and we make no speedup claim from optimization.

\emph{Conclusion validity.} Every number in the tables is produced by scripts in the archive from raw tool output; the comparisons are counts over a fixed list of configurations, with no significance tests.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a Rocq-verified optimization and export pipeline for timed B\"uchi automata, its composition with a verified MTL translation into a modular, mechanized chain whose stages are stated against interfaces, and an evaluation of the whole chain against the independent translator CASAAL. Each pass preserves the timed-word language of every input TBA, the optimizer does so for every number of rounds, and the output of export has the conjunctive guards and invariants UPPAAL requires.

On the negated requirements that a model checker receives, our automata have the same size as CASAAL's wherever both tools finish, except on three small patterns (F5, F6, F8) where ours are smaller, and over 18 products TChecker stores 204{,}564 states on ours and 204{,}819 on CASAAL's converted to location acceptance; the optimizer changes none of the counts. On the positive form of the bounded responses, composed with a system that violates the requirement and exercises every response, CASAAL's counts grow by factors of 19 to 53 per response (1{,}736{,}096 states on R4 against 114 for ours). This is a property of the generated automata on a constructed workload, it shrinks from 15{,}000 to 14 times under inclusion subsumption, and it is not an effect of the verified passes (Section~\ref{sec:casaal-stress}). The optimization passes shrink the automaton (the seven-level nested automaton falls from 10{,}930 to 5{,}105 transitions, exactly CASAAL's size) but do not change what the checkers explore; what they change is whether the checker-readable export exists within 300~s. Generation is slower than CASAAL's on nested requirements, where Spot alone takes 60 to 90\% of CASAAL's whole translation on seven levels.

What the verified chain adds is assurance that testing can miss: of 22 seeded defects in the extracted code, 18 change the language, a random-word test misses five of them, and the corresponding mistakes in the Rocq definitions are all rejected within seconds at named lemmas.

This is evidence about our own pipeline against weak baselines, not about CASAAL.

The chain is built to be changed at its front: a direct MTL-to-TBA construction in the style of CASAAL could replace the whole front end under the same theorems; whether it would close the gap on nested requirements is untested. Future work includes such a verified construction, which would also close the trust gap on Spot, and evaluation on industrial TBA corpora.
