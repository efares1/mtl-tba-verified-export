\subsection{Questions, evidence, and setup}
This section evaluates the whole translation from an MTL requirement to the automaton given to a timed model checker, and the passes inside it. Section~\ref{sec:casaal-eval} compares the translation with CASAAL in size, generation time and stored states (RQ1); Section~\ref{sec:eval-checked} evaluates the checked passes (RQ5); Section~\ref{sec:export-policies} the export policies, and Section~\ref{sec:spot-level} the Spot settings (RQ2, RQ3); Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} checks the extracted code and the exported automata against the semantics (RQ4).

We retain the 31-attempt F/R/N benchmark: F is the group of single requirement patterns from the literature, R$n$ the conjunction of $n$ bounded responses and N$n$ the nest of bounded eventualities of depth $n$; ``positive form'' is the requirement as written and ``negated'' the form given to the model checker. Of the 31 attempts, 29 completed the upstream stage and supplied an optimizer input (R7 and R8 timed out, and with the implementation of Section~\ref{sec:speedups} their positive forms complete in 14~s and 90~s). All experiments ran on Ubuntu 26.04/WSL~2, an Intel Core i7-8650U at 1.9~GHz with 11.7~GB of memory, OCaml 5.4.0, Rocq 9.0.1 and Spot 2.16, with a 300~s limit unless stated. Generation times are medians of five runs, TChecker state counts are exact, UPPAAL times are medians of three runs, and other protocols are stated where used. The generated-group numbers come from an earlier build (Spot level \texttt{small}); numbers from different builds are never added together. Across the 29 completed inputs the reachable transitions fall from 20,204 after reset completion to 1,500 after optimization (R6 accounts for 14,985 of the 18,704 removed), and export grows the explicit transitions to 3,209, a representation cost (Supplement Sections~\ref{S-sec:generated} and~\ref{S-sec:eval-extra}).

\subsection{Matched comparison of the two export policies}
\label{sec:export-policies}
Holding the optimized automaton fixed on R2--R6, all-copy export adds no explicit edges beyond the optimized relation and no diagonal guards, while maximal-delay export adds edges in each case and between 2 and 3,360 diagonal guard atoms per formula (4,464 in total); location, clock and invariant counts are the same (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:generated-export-policy}).

The policy matters to a model checker by whether it can read the automaton. TChecker's liveness algorithms (\texttt{tck-liveness -a couvscc}) reject diagonal constraints (``unsupported diagonal constraints''). On the negated requirements the two policies give identical automata and TChecker counts; on the positive forms the maximal-delay export contains diagonal guards from R2 on, and TChecker stops with this error on each, while the all-copy export is accepted. With a system that requests only the first response, TChecker stores 48 states for each of R1--R5 with the all-copy export, and 48, 375, 3{,}439 and 34{,}107 on R1--R4 with CASAAL's automaton (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:baseline-posbad}); Section~\ref{sec:casaal-stress} repeats the comparison with a system that exercises all $n$ responses.

\emph{Effect of the optimizer on the export.} The optimizer does not change what TChecker explores, but in these runs it decides whether the TChecker-readable export is produced at all. On the positive forms of R2--R6 (wall-clock times of single runs, limit 300~s; Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:export-scalability}), the all-copy export of R4 takes 44.0~s without the optimizer (625 edges) and 0.7~s with it (112 edges), and the all-copy exports of R5 and R6 do not finish in 300~s without it and finish in 5.7 and 55.5~s with it. The maximal-delay export of R6 takes 50.9~s without the optimizer and 7.9~s with it (31{,}008 against 1{,}837 edges). Only the optimized all-copy export both is accepted by TChecker's liveness algorithms and finishes on all five requirements.

\emph{Which passes are not redundant.} A leave-one-out ablation (scratch build of the extracted code whose round skips one pass; each variant still preserves the language) on the 25 fast benchmark configurations and the 100 random formulas (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:pass-ablation}) shows which passes are not redundant on these inputs, not what each contributes alone. Removing the elimination of unsatisfiable guards has the largest effect (4{,}183 transitions instead of 670 on the benchmark, 3{,}579 instead of 2{,}366 on the random formulas); removing invariant propagation or forward propagation changes none of the 125 results.

\subsection{Translator-independent synthetic suite}
\label{sec:synthetic}
To exercise the interface without the MTL translator, we run the optimizer and export on 16 small synthetic TBAs and on parameterized families written as Rocq records. On the 100 random formulas of the translation article the optimizer reduces transitions on 50 (3,980 to 2,366 in total) and increases none (Supplement Sections~\ref{S-sec:random} and~\ref{S-sec:synthetic-detail}).

\emph{Independently generated TBAs.} We generated 300 random timed B\"uchi graphs (6 to 12 locations, 2 to 4 clocks) and 300 specification-style monitors with redundant clocks, dead resets and duplicate edges, read them with \texttt{tba\_opt} and exported each with both policies. All 1{,}200 runs complete. The bounded word test of Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} (20{,}000 words, 60 loop iterations) finds no difference between an input and its optimized automaton or its exported XML; the optimizer removes 2{,}046 of the 7{,}562 transitions, on 534 of the 600 inputs. The inputs are ours, not a third-party corpus (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:independent-detail}, Table~\ref{S-tab:independent}).

\subsection{Model checking and agreement with the semantics}
\label{sec:verification-adds}
\emph{UPPAAL.} We generated 311 UPPAAL~5.0.0 models: the negated requirement in seven variants (ours with the default export, with \texttt{-n 0} and with the all-copy export; CASAAL's converted automaton plain and through our export in the same three ways) composed with small systems. All 296 runs with an expected verdict return it and the stored states are identical in all variants except on F5 and F8 (ours 3 and 2 states against 4 and 4). These are small reachability checks: they show usability and equal verdicts, not a speedup (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:uppaal-check}).

\emph{Case study.} A dual-chamber pacemaker (four timing requirements, alone and conjoined; Figure~\ref{fig:pacemaker} shows one generated automaton) and a single-threaded gateway with 2 to 6 conjoined bounded responses. Each negated requirement is translated in four ways (default, \texttt{-n 0}, all-copy, checked clock reuse), composed with a correct and with faulty systems and decided by UPPAAL; all 150 runs return the expected verdict and the timelock check holds. These negated requirements are already minimal, so the optimizer changes neither size nor stored states; checked clock reuse reduces the clocks from 4 to 1 (pacemaker) and from 6 to 1 (gateway, 6 channels). A requirement under assumptions on the environment is different: the negation of ``if each of $k$ requests is answered within 5, request 1 is followed by \emph{done} within 5'' contains the $k$ assumptions as a conjunction. UPPAAL cannot decide it (it needs a B\"uchi emptiness check), so we use TChecker with the all-copy export on the gateway. For $k=2,3,4$ and the correct system, TChecker stores 8{,}875, 132{,}438 and 1{,}706{,}718 states on our automaton with the default optimizer, as many as without it for $k=2,3$, and 20, 23 and 26 on CASAAL's. After the checked pruning and clock reuse our automaton has CASAAL's size (52, 136 and 336 transitions) and TChecker stores 18, 21 and 24 states. For $k=4$ the export without the optimizer does not finish in 300~s; for $k=5$ only the pruned automaton is produced (27 states), and TChecker does not finish on CASAAL's within 300~s. The correct system gives an empty product and the faulty system a non-empty one in every finished run. Here the default optimizer alone is far behind CASAAL, and the checked pruning closes the gap.

\emph{Agreement with the semantics.} An independent evaluator of the pointwise MTL semantics, transcribed from the Rocq definition, together with acceptance evaluators for our UPPAAL XML and for CASAAL's DOT output, was run on random ultimately periodic timed words (22 benchmark patterns with 20{,}000 words each and 100 random formulas with 6{,}000 words each, both polarities). Our tool with both export policies and CASAAL (20 patterns) agree with the evaluator on all 6{,}160{,}000 word checks (122 inputs), and the evaluator rejects 11 of 13 deliberately wrong formulas for our tool and 7 of 7 for CASAAL. This is agreement on a finite sample, and the evaluator shares its source with the proofs (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:differential}).

\emph{Hypotheses of the shipped theorem.} The shipped theorem assumes that Spot's automaton accepts the action-consistent words that satisfy the weakened clocked encoding of $\mathsf{recur}(f)$ (Section~\ref{sec:composition}), and its guarantee for UPPAAL needs the initial-value check to succeed. We tested the first hypothesis by translation validation with an independent translator (ltl2ba) and two emptiness checks with Spot's tools: of the 31 benchmark configurations, 25 validated, 6 timed out and none gave a counterexample; of the 100 random formulas, 98 validated, 2 timed out and none gave a counterexample. This is evidence about these runs and does not discharge the hypothesis. The initial-value check succeeded on F1--F14, R1--R6, N1--N5 and the case studies, and failed on 4 of the 100 random formulas, which nest lower-bounded operators such as $\Box_{\geq 1}\Diamond_{>1}\, r$, and on 423 of the 600 independently generated TBAs, which did not come from the MTL translation; the tool then warns that the result for clocks starting at 0 is not covered. These failures limit the zero-initialized reading, not preservation.

\subsection{Limitations}
TChecker states and UPPAAL verdicts are measured on small products: on the negated products, R4--R7 and N5--N6 did not finish for TChecker within 300~s, and for R8, N7 and N8 no product could be generated. CASAAL's sizes and state counts depend on our importer, which is outside the proofs. The pruning explains the large counts on the positive-form workload, which is constructed and covers one family; for that family the importer neither splits a location nor drops an edge (CASAAL prints no acceptance mark), so the overlap is in CASAAL's printed automaton, but the importer's reading of the labels as mutually exclusive letters and its expansion into one transition per disjunct are ours. Pruning is weak on generic graphs, and the timing gain of clock reuse is measured on constructed families with one checker. The theorems cover words that are infinite, time-divergent and have one event per position; the UPPAAL checks are reachability of an accepting sink and no theorem relates the exported automaton to the semantics of UPPAAL or TChecker. The slowdown on deep nests rests on one profile of N7, and we built no alternative front end.
