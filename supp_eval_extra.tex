\subsection{Generation time on deep nests: profile and the Spot-level trade-off}
\label{sec:deep-nests}
\emph{Where the time goes on deep nests.} A profile of Spot on the simplified
formula of N7 (12.9~s of processor time, 43~MB) attributes 79\% of its time to
its BDD library and 16\% to the rest of Spot. None of the options of
\texttt{spot-x(7)} that we tried (implication-based formula simplification,
formula splitting, SCC filtering, explicit iteration over valuations, Boolean
relabeling, branching postponement) changed the time beyond the run-to-run
spread, and compositional suspension doubled it. On this input the cost may lie in the formula
that the verified translation hands to Spot: a bounded eventuality repeats
the translation of its operand three times, so the formula has size $\Theta(3^k)$ in the
nesting depth $k$ (the proposition $p_7$ occurs $2{,}187=3^7$ times in the
formula of N7). Spot's time grows by a factor of roughly 4 to 12 per level on the levels measured while its
output (129 states and 4{,}623 edges at seven levels) grows by about 3.5. On N7,
Spot alone (10--13~s) takes about as long as CASAAL's whole translation
(11.3~s), and the rest of our chain takes about 5~s. On N7 the difference therefore appears to lie in
the replaceable front end (article Section~\ref{sec:modular}) and not in the verified
optimization and export; this rests on one profile, and we did not profile N8.

\emph{Why the default is a trade-off.} Against the better explicit setting,
automatic mode loses 0.6~s on N5 (where \texttt{low} is simply the slower
setting), 5.3~s on N6 and 15.6~s on N7 (the 5~s of the abandoned \texttt{low}
attempt plus run-to-run variation that we did not separate), and it avoids the
minutes that \texttt{low} alone needs on N7 (393~s in a separate run) and the
more than 900~s it needs on N8. On the conjunctions it is never
worse than the former default \texttt{small}, which needs 41~s on seven
responses and fails on eight. On the \emph{positive} forms, which one would give
to a model checker as an assumption, automatic mode costs little: seven
conjoined bounded responses take 14.0~s with \texttt{low}, 8.7~s with
\texttt{any} and 14.3~s in automatic mode, and eight take 89.9~s in automatic
mode, where neither explicit setting finished within the 30~s limit of the
earlier comparison; CASAAL's own times on the positive forms were not measured.
On mixed formulas, nested chains conjoined with plain responses
(Section~\ref{sec:spot-level}), \texttt{any} is at worst 7.7~s where \texttt{low}
needs 0.3~s, which automatic mode avoids by trying \texttt{low} first, and
\texttt{low} exceeds 30~s on nested conjunctions of depth five where \texttt{any}
needs 0.2~s, which automatic mode reaches after its budget.

\subsection{A probe of location merging}
The conclusion lists a pass that merges equivalent locations as future work. As a lower bound on what it could gain on the benchmarks we computed, on the exported negated automaton, the coarsest syntactic bisimulation (equal accepting mark and invariant, and equal outgoing (event, guard, resets, class of target)); it is sound for the timed language but not complete for timed bisimulation (script \path{bisim_probe.py}). It merges no location of any of the 23 automata probed (F1--F13 with F3b, R1--R4, N1--N5; TChecker counts exist for 20 of them); it only removes a duplicate edge on F2 (7 to 6 edges) and F12 (17 to 16), and the TChecker counts do not change. The verified optimizer is therefore at the syntactic bisimulation fixed point on these inputs. This says nothing about a semantic (zone-based) merge, which we did not try.

The agreement above shows that the tools are correct on the sample, not what the proofs add. To measure that, we seeded 22 plausible defects, one at a time, in a scratch copy of the extracted optimizer and exporter (Table~\ref{tab:mutants}; for instance a strict instead of non-strict test at the bound zero, a reset set not compared when two locations are merged, the complement of $x<c$ taken as $x>c$). Each mutant was tested in five ways: T1 compares its output with that of the unmutated tool on the benchmark and the corpus of 148 generated automata; T2 tests, for up to 100{,}000 random words per input, whether the output automaton accepts the words of its input; T3 (the fuzz) repeats this on 3{,}000 random automata drawn with the same generator and another seed (a corpus about 20 times larger than the 148, with small constants including zero), testing 5{,}000 words on each output that differs from the unmutated tool's; T4 compares each such output with its input on every lasso word of at most four positions (prefix 0--3, loop 1--3) over all events, with delays in $\{0,0.5,1,2,5\}$ time units and every initial clock valuation with values in $\{0,1,2,3,5\}$, which is exact on that finite word set only; and T5 is a structure-directed fuzz on 1{,}500 further random automata in which each guarded transition receives, with probability one half, a twin with the same source, target, label and resets whose guard is the complement of one atom (for a strict bound, in half of the cases the strict opposite bound, which holds on neither side of $x=c$). Only outputs that differ are tested, because an output byte-identical to the unmutated tool's has the language of the unmutated tool, which the proofs equate with the input's.

Eighteen of the 22 mutants change the language of some automaton; four do not (M05 changes the output but not the language; M08, M21 and M22 change neither). Thirteen changes are found by T2. Five, M01, M10, M12, M16 and M17, are found by neither T1 nor T2: the benchmark and the small corpus never reach the defect (four of the five change no output at all), and the word test, which fails to notice even a single dropped transition in 36\% of our controls, does not hit the few words that separate the languages. T3 finds M01, M10, M16 and M17, on 44, 2, 31 and 5 inputs (an automaton under one export policy; 6{,}000 inputs in all), and T4 the same four, on 18, 1, 27 and 1 of the inputs whose output changes; the one mismatch for M17 is a single word, so M17 does change the language, although T2 alone did not detect it. M12 changes no output on any of the 3{,}000 automata, so T3 and T4 cannot see it; T5 finds it, with a changed output on 1{,}020 of 1{,}500 automata and a language mismatch on 164 (M01, M16, M17 on 26, 16 and 2; M10 on none). For M01 and M12 we built an automaton on which the mutant's language differs (for M01, 852 accepted sample words become none; for M12, 3{,}234 against 3{,}564), so M12 is a real language change that every test of this kind missed. The test oracles are themselves unverified code, T4 is exact only on its word set, and no exact language-equivalence check was tried. M12 was found only by a generator written with that pass in mind, which is the point: a test finds a defect when someone thinks of it.

We then applied the same mistakes to the Rocq definitions in a scratch copy of the development (control: the unmutated copy compiles in about 43 s with the four standard axioms). All 22 are rejected by Rocq within 1.8 to 9.1 seconds at a named lemma (Table~\ref{tab:rocq-rejection}). The rejection is at the lemma that ties the implementation to its specification (for M01, against the specification function of the invariants), so the check is only as strong as that specification: a mutation that changed implementation and specification together would pass. It is also conservative: it rejects the four mutants that do not change the language, so a rejection means that the definition no longer matches the specification, not that its language differs. The Rocq mutants are transcriptions of the extracted-code mutants, made by the authors. These results make a bounded claim: for these defects in this code, T1 and T2 missed five of 18 language-changing defects, a corpus about 20 times larger and an exhaustive short-word check missed one, a generator aimed at the merge pass found it, and the development rejects all of them. They do not show that CASAAL contains such defects, and CASAAL may well be correct.

\subsection{Generated group: setup details and aggregates}
This subsection gives the setup details and the aggregates behind article Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. Internal output shape is a Rocq theorem; external checker verdicts, sizes and times are experimental evidence. The matched TChecker cases below show unchanged exploration on systems whose exported models are byte-identical before and after optimization; they do not establish a general cost improvement.

We retain the 31-attempt F/R/N benchmark as an upstream-generated
group. The optimizer input is the automaton after reset completion, shown as
Comp. in Table~\ref{tab:optimizer-benchmarks}. Of the 31 attempted
configurations, 29 completed this upstream stage and supplied an optimizer
input; R7 and R8 timed out before the measured boundary (with the later
implementation of article Section~\ref{sec:speedups}, their positive forms complete in
14 s and 90 s). Formula rewriting, Spot
configuration, cube expansion, relaxation, and reset completion happen
upstream; their effects are not credited to the optimizer. The runs use the
tool's default front end, which before translation rewrites
$\Box\Diamond_{\ge d}\varphi$ into $\Box\Diamond\varphi$ and
$\Diamond\Box_{\ge d}\varphi$ into $\Diamond\Box\varphi$, and which replaces
the Until of the clauses of upper-bounded hatted Until by a weak Until. The
end-to-end theorem for this front end is
\path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_recur_weak_with}
(\path{MTL_to_TBA_Recur.v}); the optimizer theorems are unaffected, since
they hold for every input TBA of the interface. Results
are medians of five runs per completed configuration, with a 300~s limit per
attempt, on Ubuntu 26.04/WSL~2, an Intel Core i7-8650U at 1.9~GHz with

\path{evaluation/runs/2026-10-14-full-eval-v3/}.
