\subsection{Questions, evidence, and setup}
This section evaluates the whole translation from an MTL requirement to the automaton given to a timed model checker, and the optimization passes inside it. Section~\ref{sec:casaal-eval} compares the translation, tool to tool, with CASAAL in the size of the automaton, in generation time and in the states a timed model checker stores (RQ1). Section~\ref{sec:export-policies} measures the export and the two export policies, and Section~\ref{sec:spot-level} the Spot settings (RQ2, RQ3). Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} checks the exported automata against the semantics and asks what verification adds that testing does not (RQ4). Internal output shape is a Rocq theorem; checker verdicts, sizes and times are experimental evidence.

We retain the 31-attempt F/R/N benchmark as an upstream-generated group. F is the group of single requirement patterns from the literature, R$n$ the conjunction of $n$ bounded responses and N$n$ the nest of bounded eventualities of depth $n$; ``positive form'' is the requirement as written and ``negated'' the form given to the model checker. Of the 31 attempts, 29 configurations completed the upstream stage and supplied an optimizer input (R7 and R8 timed out, and with the later implementation of Section~\ref{sec:speedups} their positive forms complete in 14~s and 90~s). The end-to-end theorem for the default front end is \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_recur_weak_with}; the optimizer theorems hold for every input TBA of the interface. All experiments ran on Ubuntu 26.04/WSL~2, an Intel Core i7-8650U at 1.9~GHz with 11.7~GB of memory, OCaml 5.4.0, Rocq 9.0.1 and Spot 2.16, with a 300~s limit unless stated. Generation times are medians of five runs (Table~\ref{tab:same-driver}), TChecker state counts are exact, UPPAAL times are medians of three runs, and other protocols are stated where used. The generated-group numbers were measured with Spot level \texttt{small} and the implementation before the speedups; the stress tables and the automatic-mode times come from later builds, and numbers from different builds are never added together. The run log and generated automata are under \path{evaluation/runs/2026-10-14-full-eval-v3/}.

Across the 29 completed inputs the reachable transitions fall from 20,204 after reset completion to 1,500 after optimization (92.58\%), but this is skewed: R6 accounts for 14,985 of the 18,704 removed. Export then grows the explicit transitions from 1,500 to 3,209, a representation cost. The per-instance and aggregate tables are in Supplement Sections~\ref{S-sec:generated} and~\ref{S-sec:eval-extra}.

\subsection{Matched comparison of the two export policies}
\label{sec:export-policies}
Holding the optimized automaton fixed on R2--R6, all-copy export adds no explicit edges beyond the optimized relation and no diagonal guards, while maximal-delay export adds edges in each case and between 2 and 3,360 diagonal guard atoms per formula (4,464 in total); location, clock and invariant counts are the same, and exporter times have no consistent order (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:generated-export-policy}). This is a property of this benchmark family, not a general size theorem.

The policy matters to a model checker by whether it can read the automaton. TChecker's liveness algorithms (\texttt{tck-liveness -a couvscc}) reject diagonal constraints (``unsupported diagonal constraints''). On the negated requirements the two policies give identical automata and TChecker counts; on the positive forms the maximal-delay export contains diagonal guards from R2 on, and TChecker stops with this error on each, while the all-copy export is accepted. With a system that violates the requirement on every run and requests only the first response, so that the product is empty and explored completely, TChecker stores 48 states for each of R1--R5 with the all-copy export, and 48, 375, 3{,}439 and 34{,}107 on R1--R4 with CASAAL's automaton converted to location acceptance (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:baseline-posbad}). This system exercises one response whatever $n$ is; Section~\ref{sec:casaal-stress} repeats the comparison with a system that exercises all $n$ responses, where the difference grows with $n$; neither the optimizer nor the export removes it.


\emph{Effect of the optimizer on the export.} The optimizer does not change what TChecker explores, but in these runs it decides whether the TChecker-readable export is produced at all. The times in this paragraph and in Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:export-scalability} are wall-clock times of the complete run of the tool (Spot, reset completion, optimization, initial-value check, export and writing the output), not of the export stage alone as in Section~\ref{sec:speedups}. On the positive forms of R2--R6, with one run each, a limit of 300~s and the build of that run, the all-copy export of R4 takes 44.0~s without the optimizer (625 edges) and 0.7~s with it (112 edges), and the all-copy exports of R5 and R6 (five and six responses) do not finish in 300~s without it and finish in 5.7 and 55.5~s with it (single runs on one machine; the second time is close enough to the limit that run-to-run variation could matter). The maximal-delay export of R6 takes 50.9~s without the optimizer and 7.9~s with it (31{,}008 against 1{,}837 edges). Of the four configurations, only the optimized all-copy export both is accepted by TChecker's liveness algorithms and finishes on all five requirements in these runs. Without the optimizer, the edge count of the maximal-delay export grows by a factor of 5 to 6 per added response (28, 163, 950, 5{,}469 and 31{,}008).

\emph{Which passes are not redundant.} A leave-one-out ablation shows which passes are not redundant on these inputs, not what each contributes alone, because a pass whose effect another pass reproduces shows no change. We ran the optimizer with one pass removed at a time, in a scratch build of the extracted code whose round skips that pass (each variant still preserves the language, since every pass is proved separately), on the requirements as written for the 25 benchmark configurations whose Spot stage is fast (F1--F14, R1--R5, N1--N5) and the 100 random formulas (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:pass-ablation}). Among the variants, removing the elimination of unsatisfiable guards has the largest effect on the transition count: the optimized automata have 4{,}183 transitions instead of 670 on the benchmark, and 3{,}579 instead of 2{,}366 on the random formulas. Removing guard normalization, dead-reset removal or clock merging leaves up to 21 more clocks on the random formulas, and removing transition merging adds 25 transitions on the benchmark and 329 on the random formulas. Removing invariant propagation or forward bound propagation changes none of the 125 results, and removing the elimination of contradictory guards or of unreachable locations changes one and two of them.

\subsection{Translator-independent synthetic suite}
\label{sec:synthetic}
To exercise the interface without the MTL translator, we run the optimizer and export on 16 small synthetic TBAs and on parameterized families written as Rocq records. They cover redundant and contradictory guards, dead resets, synchronously reset clocks, duplicate transitions, equivalent locations and a disjunctive-invariant export. It shows that each pass acts on the structure it targets and that the passes interact: omitting forward propagation retains all seven edges of the forward cascade where the full optimizer leaves four (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:synthetic-interactions}). On the 100 random formulas of the translation article the optimizer reduces transitions on 50 (3,980 to 2,366 in total) and increases none (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:random}; further tables in Section~\ref{S-sec:synthetic-detail}).
\emph{Independently generated TBAs.} To test the passes on automata that no MTL translator produced, we generated 300 random timed B\"uchi graphs (6 to 12 locations, 2 to 4 clocks) and 300 specification-style monitors (response chains, periodic events, watchdogs, rate limiters and windows, with redundant clocks, dead resets and unreachable or duplicate edges), read them with \texttt{tba\_opt} and exported each with both policies (\path{evaluation/independent/}). All 1{,}200 runs complete. The bounded word test of Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} (20{,}000 words, six initial valuations, 60 loop iterations) finds no difference between an input and its optimized automaton or its exported XML; the optimizer removes 2{,}046 of the 7{,}562 transitions in total, on 534 of the 600 inputs. A first run with 12 iterations flagged one input through a finite-horizon artifact that disappears with 60. The check \path{init_free} fails on 423 of the 600 inputs, for which only the existential initial valuation is tested. The inputs are ours, not a third-party corpus.



\subsection{Model checking, agreement with the semantics, and what verification adds}
\label{sec:verification-adds}
\emph{UPPAAL.} We generated 311 UPPAAL~5.0.0 models: the negated requirement in seven variants (ours with the default export, with \texttt{-n 0} (optimizer off) and with the all-copy export; CASAAL's automaton converted to location acceptance, plain and through our export in the same three ways) composed with request/response, parallel-component, sporadic and free systems. All 296 runs with an expected verdict return it, and UPPAAL reads the default export with its diagonal guards. On full explorations the stored states are identical in all variants except on the products of F5 and F8, where ours stores 3 and 2 states against 4 and 4. In the one case we examined, the times follow the number of explicit property edges: R6 with parallel components takes 1.3~s on ours (182 edges) and 3.5~s on CASAAL's (330 edges). These are small systems and reachability queries, so they show usability and equal verdicts, not a speedup (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:uppaal-check}, Table~\ref{S-tab:uppaal}). For timed liveness, the matched TChecker runs with and without the optimizer store the same states (77 and 15 states on the two cases).

\emph{Agreement with the semantics.} An independent evaluator of the pointwise MTL semantics, transcribed from the Rocq definition, together with acceptance evaluators for our UPPAAL XML and for CASAAL's DOT output, was run on random ultimately periodic timed words (22 benchmark patterns with 20{,}000 words each and 100 random formulas with 6{,}000 words each, both polarities). Our tool with both export policies (22 patterns) and CASAAL (20 patterns; F3 and F13 use hatted operators) agree with the evaluator on all 6{,}160{,}000 word checks (122 inputs). The evaluator rejects 11 of 13 deliberately wrong formulas for our tool and 7 of 7 for CASAAL. This finds no bug in either tool; it is agreement on a finite sample, and the evaluator shares its source with the proofs, so it cannot detect an error in the specification itself (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:differential}).

\emph{Hypotheses of the shipped theorem.} The shipped theorem assumes that the automaton returned by Spot accepts the action-consistent words that satisfy the weakened clocked encoding of $\mathsf{recur}(f)$ (Section~\ref{sec:composition}), and its guarantee for UPPAAL needs the initial-value check to succeed. We tested the first hypothesis on the automata Spot returned, by translation validation. The script validates the formula that the tool actually passes to Spot. An independent translator (ltl2ba) builds automata for that formula and for its negation, and two emptiness checks with Spot's tools show that Spot's automaton accepts exactly the models of the formula (\path{evaluation/run_spot_validation.sh}). The checks do not restrict to action-consistent words, so a success establishes the hypothesis, which asks for less. The check succeeds on 25 of the 31 benchmark configurations and on 98 of the 100 random formulas. On R6--R8 and N6--N8 ltl2ba did not finish in 300~s, and on two random formulas the equivalence check did not finish in 120~s; no check failed, so the hypothesis is untested on the largest inputs. This is evidence about these runs, outside the proofs, and it trusts ltl2ba and the emptiness checks. The initial-value check succeeded on F1--F14, R1--R6 and N1--N5 in both polarities and on the three case-study requirements, and failed on 4 of the 100 random formulas; the failing formulas nest lower-bounded operators, such as $\Box_{\geq 1}\Diamond_{>1}\, r$, and the tool then warns that the result for clocks starting at 0 is not covered by the theorem.

\emph{Seeded defects.} Agreement does not show what the proofs add. We therefore injected 22 plausible defects of our choosing, one at a time, in a scratch copy of the extracted optimizer and exporter (a strict instead of non-strict test at the bound zero, a reset set not compared when two locations merge, the complement of $x<c$ taken as $x>c$) and tested each in five ways. T1 compares the output with the unmutated tool's on the benchmark and 148 generated automata. T2 tests, on up to 100{,}000 random words per input, whether the output accepts the words of its input. T3 repeats T1 and T2 on 3{,}000 random automata. T4 compares each differing output with its input on every lasso word of at most three positions (exact on that word set only). T5 is a structure-directed fuzz that gives half of the guarded transitions of 1{,}500 random automata a twin transition with the complemented guard. Eighteen mutants change the language of some automaton, and T2 finds 13 of them. Five (M01, M10, M12, M16, M17) pass T1 and T2. T3 and T4 find M01, M10, M16 and M17; M12 changes no output on any of the 3{,}000 random automata, and T5, which we wrote after M12 escaped, finds it (a language mismatch on 164 of 1{,}500 automata). After we added T5, every language-changing mutant is found by some test, but M12 only by a generator aimed at the pass that it breaks. The other four mutants (M05, M08, M21, M22) show no language change under T3 and T4; M05 is a different composition of verified passes, and the other three are argued, not proved, to preserve the language.

The word test is a weak baseline: it misses even a single dropped transition in 36\% of our controls (354 of 553 detected), and we tried no exact language-equivalence check. We applied the same mistakes to the Rocq definitions in a scratch copy of the development, which compiles unmutated in about 43~s. The development rejects all 22 transcribed mutants within 1.8 to 9.1 seconds, each at a named lemma (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:rocq-rejection}), including the five that T1 and T2 missed and the four that do not change the language: a rejection means that the mutated definition no longer matches the specification the lemmas are stated against, not that its language differs. The Rocq mutants are our transcriptions, and the check is as strong as the specification it compares against: a mutation of implementation and specification together would pass. The claim is therefore bounded: for these defects in this code, the proofs reject all 22 without a test designed for each, while T1 and T2 missed five of the 18 language-changing ones. It does not show a defect in CASAAL, which agreed with the evaluator throughout (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:mutation}, Tables~\ref{S-tab:mutants} and~\ref{S-tab:rocq-rejection}).

\subsection{Limitations}
TChecker states and UPPAAL verdicts are measured on small products: on the negated products, R4--R7 and N5--N6 did not finish for TChecker within 300~s, and for R8, N7 and N8 no product could be generated. CASAAL's sizes and state counts depend on our importer, which is outside the proofs. The difference on the positive-form workload is located in the zone graph of CASAAL's automaton, but its cause is not determined (the conversion is not excluded), and the workload is constructed. The mutation study uses 22 hand-chosen defects in the extracted code, unverified test oracles and author-made Rocq transcriptions; we kept no log of real bugs that the proofs exposed. The theorems cover words that are infinite, time-divergent and have one event per position; the UPPAAL checks are reachability of an accepting sink, not B\"uchi checks, and are not covered by the theorems; no theorem relates the exported automaton to the semantics of UPPAAL or TChecker. The check \path{init_free} fails on 4 of 100 random formulas, for which the zero-initialized reading is not established. The slowdown on deep nests is consistent with Spot's translation of a clocked formula of size $\Theta(3^k)$, but rests on one profile of N7, and we built no alternative front end.
