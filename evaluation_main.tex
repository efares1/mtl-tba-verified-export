\subsection{Questions, evidence, and setup}
This section evaluates the whole translation from an MTL requirement to the automaton given to a timed model checker, and the optimization passes inside it. Section~\ref{sec:casaal-eval} compares the translation, tool to tool, with CASAAL in the size of the automaton, in generation time and in the states a timed model checker stores (RQ1). Section~\ref{sec:export-policies} measures the export and the two export policies, and Section~\ref{sec:spot-level} the Spot settings (RQ2, RQ3). Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} checks the exported automata against the semantics and asks what verification adds that testing does not (RQ4). Internal output shape is a Rocq theorem; checker verdicts, sizes and times are experimental evidence.

We retain the 31-attempt F/R/N benchmark as an upstream-generated group. F is the group of single requirement patterns from the literature, R$n$ the conjunction of $n$ bounded responses and N$n$ the nest of bounded eventualities of depth $n$; ``positive form'' is the requirement as written and ``negated'' the form given to the model checker. Of the 31 attempts, 29 configurations completed the upstream stage and supplied an optimizer input (R7 and R8 timed out, and with the later implementation of Section~\ref{sec:speedups} their positive forms complete in 14~s and 90~s). Rewriting, Spot configuration, cube expansion and reset completion happen upstream and are not credited to the optimizer. The end-to-end theorem for the default front end is \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_recur_weak_with}; the optimizer theorems hold for every input TBA of the interface. All experiments ran on Ubuntu 26.04/WSL~2, an Intel Core i7-8650U at 1.9~GHz with 11.7~GB of memory, OCaml 5.4.0, Rocq 9.0.1 and Spot 2.16, with a 300~s limit unless stated. The protocol depends on the experiment and is stated where the result is reported: generation times in Table~\ref{tab:same-driver} are medians of five runs, TChecker state counts are exact and need no repetition, UPPAAL times are medians of three runs, and the generated-group tables of the supplement give medians of five runs per completed configuration (only the CASAAL generation table of the supplement gives single runs of ours). The generated-group numbers were measured with Spot level \texttt{small} and the implementation before the speedups; the run log and generated automata are under \path{evaluation/runs/2026-10-14-full-eval-v3/}.

Across the 29 completed inputs the reachable transitions fall from 20,204 after reset completion to 1,500 after optimization (92.58\%), but this is skewed: R6 accounts for 14,985 of the 18,704 removed, and the six bounded-response R cases for 98.6\%. Export then grows the explicit transitions from 1,500 to 3,209, a representation cost reported separately. With symbolic grouping in both runs, the optimizer alone lowers the symbolic transitions from 704 to 344 on the 27 configurations whose unoptimized export completes (smaller on 17, equal on 10). The largest case is R6: 64 Spot states and 15,625 cube-split transitions become 640 edges and six clocks, and export produces 1,837 explicit edges with 63 invariant-bearing locations. The per-instance and aggregate tables are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:generated} and Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:eval-extra} (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:optimizer-aggregates}).

\subsection{Matched comparison of the two export policies}
\label{sec:export-policies}
Holding the optimized automaton fixed on R2--R6, all-copy export adds no explicit edges beyond the optimized relation and no diagonal guards, while maximal-delay export adds edges in each case and between 2 and 3,360 diagonal guard atoms per formula (4,464 in total); location, clock and invariant counts are the same, and exporter times have no consistent order (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:generated-export-policy}). This is a property of this benchmark family, not a general size theorem.

The policy matters to a model checker by whether it can read the automaton. TChecker's liveness algorithms (\texttt{tck-liveness -a couvscc}) reject diagonal constraints (``unsupported diagonal constraints''). On the negated requirements the two policies give identical automata and TChecker counts; on the positive forms the maximal-delay export contains diagonal guards from R2 on, and TChecker stops with this error on each, while the all-copy export is accepted. We composed the positive automaton of R$n$ with a system that violates the requirement on every run, so that the product is empty and explored completely. With the all-copy export TChecker stores 48 states for each of R1--R5 (52 without the invariants of the export); with CASAAL's automaton, converted to location acceptance, it stores 48, 375, 3{,}439 and 34{,}107 on R1--R4 (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:baseline-posbad}). That system, however, makes a request for the first response only, so it exercises one response whatever $n$ is, and the constant 48 says nothing about $n$. Section~ef{sec:casaal-stress} repeats the comparison with a system that exercises all $n$ responses; there the difference to CASAAL's automaton grows with $n$ and is not caused by the optimizer or the export.


\emph{What the optimizer buys the export.} The optimizer does not change what TChecker explores, but it decides whether the TChecker-readable export is produced at all. On the positive forms of R2--R6, with one run each, a limit of 300~s and the current build, the all-copy export of R4 takes 44.0~s without the optimizer (625 edges) and 0.7~s with it (112 edges), and those of R5 and R6 do not finish in 300~s without it and finish in 5.7 and 55.5~s with it. The maximal-delay export of R6 takes 50.9~s without the optimizer and 7.9~s with it (31{,}008 against 1{,}837 edges). Of the four configurations, only the optimized all-copy export both is accepted by TChecker's liveness algorithms and finishes on all five requirements (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:export-scalability}). We did not profile why the unoptimized export is slow; its edge count grows by a factor of 5 to 6 per added response (28, 163, 950, 5{,}469 and 31{,}008 for the maximal-delay export), and the times are single runs on one machine.

\emph{Which passes matter.} To see which verified pass changes the result, we ran the optimizer with one pass removed at a time, in a scratch build of the extracted code whose round skips that pass (each variant still preserves the language, since every pass is proved separately). The inputs were the requirements as written for the 25 benchmark configurations whose Spot stage is fast (F1--F14, R1--R5, N1--N5) and the 100 random formulas (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:pass-ablation}). Removing the elimination of unsatisfiable guards has the largest effect: the optimized automata have 4{,}183 transitions instead of 670 on the benchmark, and 3{,}579 instead of 2{,}366 on the random formulas. Removing guard normalization, dead-reset removal or clock merging leaves up to 21 more clocks on the random formulas (131 against 152 without dead-reset removal), and removing transition merging adds 25 transitions on the benchmark and 329 on the random formulas. Removing invariant propagation or forward bound propagation changes none of the 125 results, and removing the elimination of contradictory guards or of unreachable locations changes one and two of them. This is a leave-one-out experiment on an iterated fixed point, so a pass whose effect another pass reproduces shows no change; it shows which passes are not redundant on these inputs, not what each contributes alone. The synthetic suite below isolates the passes that do nothing here.

\subsection{Translator-independent synthetic suite}
\label{sec:synthetic}
To exercise the interface without the MTL translator, we run the optimizer and export on 16 small synthetic TBAs and on parameterized families written as Rocq records. They cover redundant and contradictory guards, dead resets, synchronously reset clocks, duplicate transitions, equivalent locations and a disjunctive-invariant export. The suite is synthetic and is no evidence of industrial performance; it illustrates that each pass acts on the structure it targets and that the passes interact: omitting forward propagation retains all seven edges of the forward cascade where the full optimizer leaves four, and normalizing a tautological $x\ge0$ guard is what lets dead-reset removal drop a clock (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:synthetic-interactions}). On the 100 random formulas of the translation article the optimizer reduces transitions on 50 (3,980 to 2,366 in total) and increases none (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:random}); the remaining tables are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:synthetic-detail}.


\subsection{Model checking, agreement with the semantics, and what verification adds}
\label{sec:verification-adds}
\emph{UPPAAL.} We generated 311 UPPAAL~5.0.0 models: the negated requirement in seven variants (ours with the default, \texttt{-n 0} and all-copy exports; CASAAL's automaton converted to location acceptance, plain and through our export in the same three ways) composed with request/response, parallel-component, sporadic and free systems. All 296 runs with an expected verdict return it, for every variant, and UPPAAL reads the default export with its diagonal guards. On full explorations the stored states are identical in all variants except on the products of F5 and F8, where ours stores 3 and 2 states against 4 and 4. Where times differ they follow the number of explicit property edges: R6 with parallel components takes 1.3~s on ours (182 edges) and 3.5~s on CASAAL's (330 edges), and CASAAL's automaton passed through our export is as slow as its own. These are small systems and reachability queries, so they show that the exported models are usable and give the same verdicts, not a general speedup (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:uppaal-check}, Table~\ref{S-tab:uppaal}). For timed liveness, the matched TChecker runs with and without the optimizer store the same states and explore the same product (77 and 15 states on the two cases).

\emph{Agreement with the semantics.} An independent evaluator of the pointwise MTL semantics, transcribed from the Rocq definition, together with acceptance evaluators for our UPPAAL XML and for CASAAL's DOT output, was run on random ultimately periodic timed words (22 benchmark patterns with 20{,}000 words each and 100 random formulas with 6{,}000 words each, both polarities). Our tool with both export policies and CASAAL agree with the evaluator on all 6{,}160{,}000 word checks (22 patterns $\times$ 20{,}000 words $\times$ 2 polarities, for each of our two policies, and the same for CASAAL on the 20 patterns it encodes; 100 formulas $\times$ 6{,}000 words $\times$ 2 polarities for each of the three automata). The evaluator rejects 11 of 13 deliberately wrong formulas for our tool and 7 of 7 for CASAAL; it accepts the other two for our tool. This finds no bug in either tool; it is agreement on a finite sample of a restricted word class, and the evaluator shares its source, the Rocq definition, with the proofs, so it cannot detect an error in the specification itself (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:differential}).

\emph{Hypotheses of the shipped theorem.} The shipped theorem assumes that the automaton returned by Spot accepts exactly the propositional models of $T(f)$, and its guarantee for UPPAAL needs the initial-value check to succeed. We tested the first hypothesis on the automata Spot actually returned, by translation validation: an independent translator (ltl2ba) builds automata for $T(f)$ and for its negation, and two emptiness checks with Spot's tools show that Spot's automaton accepts only models of $T(f)$ and every model (\path{evaluation/run_spot_validation.sh}). The check succeeds on 25 of the 31 benchmark configurations and on 98 of the 100 random formulas. On R6--R8 and N6--N8 ltl2ba did not finish in 300~s, and on two random formulas the equivalence check did not finish in 120~s; no check failed. This is evidence about these runs, outside the proofs, and it trusts ltl2ba, the renaming of atoms and the emptiness checks. The initial-value check succeeded on F1--F14, R1--R6 and N1--N5 in both polarities and on the three case-study requirements, and failed on 4 of the 100 random formulas (5 in the earlier run archived as \path{random_opt.tsv}); the failing formulas nest lower-bounded operators, such as $\Box_{\geq 1}\Diamond_{>1}\, r$, and the tool then warns that the result for clocks starting at 0 is not covered by the theorem.

\emph{Seeded defects.} Agreement does not show what the proofs add. We therefore injected 22 plausible defects, one at a time, in a scratch copy of the extracted optimizer and exporter (a strict instead of non-strict test at the bound zero, a reset set not compared when two locations merge, the complement of $x<c$ taken as $x>c$) and tested each in five ways. T1 compares its output with the unmutated tool's on the benchmark and a corpus of 148 generated automata. T2 tests, on up to 100{,}000 random words per input, whether the output accepts the words of its input. T3 repeats T1 and T2 on 3{,}000 random automata, testing 5{,}000 words on each output that differs from the unmutated tool's. T4 compares each such output with its input on every lasso word of at most three positions, with delays in $\{0,0.5,1,2,5\}$ and initial clock values in $\{0,1,2,3,5\}$; it is exact on that word set only. T5 is a structure-directed fuzz: on 1{,}500 random automata, half of the guarded transitions receive a twin transition whose guard is the complement of one atom or, for a strict bound, the strict opposite bound that holds neither side of $x=c$. Eighteen mutants change the language of some automaton. T2 finds 13 of them. Five, M01, M10, M12, M16 and M17, pass T1 and T2: the corpus and the benchmark rarely reach the defect, and the word test does not hit the few words that separate the languages. T3 finds M01, M10, M16 and M17 (on 44, 2, 31 and 5 inputs) and T4 finds the same four (on 18, 1, 27 and 1 of the inputs on which the output changes). M12 changes no output on any of the 3{,}000 random automata of T3, so T3 and T4 cannot see it; T5 finds it, with 1{,}020 changed outputs and a language mismatch on 164 of 1{,}500 automata. Every language-changing mutant is therefore found by some test, but M12 only by a generator aimed at the pass that it breaks. The other four mutants (M05, M08, M21, M22) show no language change under T3 and T4; M05 is a different composition of verified passes, and the others are argued, not proved.

The word test is a weak baseline: it misses even a single dropped transition in 36\% of our controls (354 of 553 detected), and we tried no exact language-equivalence check. To see what the proofs add, we applied the same mistakes to the Rocq definitions in a scratch copy of the development, which compiles unmutated in about 43~s with the four standard axioms. The development rejects all 22 transcribed mutants within 1.8 to 9.1 seconds, each at a named lemma (Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:rocq-rejection}), including the five that T1 and T2 missed. It also rejects the four that do not change the language: a rejection means that the mutated definition no longer matches the specification the lemmas are stated against, not that its language differs, so the check is conservative. The Rocq mutants are our transcriptions of the extracted-code mutants, and the check is as strong as the specification it compares against: a mutation of implementation and specification together would pass. The claim is therefore bounded: for these defects in this code, T1 and T2 missed five of 18 language-changing defects, a larger random corpus and an exhaustive grid found four of them, a generator aimed at the merge pass found the last, and the development rejects all of them without a test designed for each. It does not show a defect in CASAAL, which agreed with the evaluator throughout (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:mutation}, Tables~\ref{S-tab:mutants} and~\ref{S-tab:rocq-rejection}).

\subsection{Limitations}
TChecker states and UPPAAL verdicts are measured on small products: R4--R7 and N5--N6 did not finish for TChecker within 300~s, and for R8, N7 and N8 no product could be generated. The importer that converts CASAAL's acceptance is outside the proofs, and the difference on the positive-form workload is not explained. The mutation study uses 22 hand-chosen defects, mutants of the extracted code and not of the Rocq sources, and unverified test oracles; the Rocq-level mutants are transcriptions made by the authors. We kept no log of the defects that proof development exposed, so the study cannot say how many real bugs the proofs found. The theorems cover words that are infinite, time-divergent and have one event per position; the UPPAAL checks are reachability of an accepting sink, not B\"uchi checks, and are not covered by the theorems. The check \texttt{init\_free} fails on 4 of 100 random formulas, for which the zero-initialized reading is not established. The slowdown on deep nests is consistent with Spot's translation of a clocked formula of size $\Theta(3^k)$ for depth $k$, but this rests on one profile of N7, and we built no alternative front end. The theorems are universal only over the Rocq TBA interface and its timed-word semantics, not through an unverified adapter to arbitrary file formats.
