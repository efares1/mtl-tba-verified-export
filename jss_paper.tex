\begin{abstract}
MTL requirements are checked by translating their negation into a timed B\"uchi automaton (TBA) that UPPAAL or TChecker composes with the system. We present an MTL-to-TBA chain whose optimization and export stages are verified in Rocq. The passes preserve the timed-word language of every TBA over the formula-indexed clock interface, for any number of optimization rounds. Two verified policies eliminate disjunctive location invariants; the all-copy policy introduces no diagonal guards, so TChecker's liveness algorithms accept its automata. An end-to-end theorem adds a verified MTL translation. The shipped tool runs Spot, so its guarantee needs a stated hypothesis on Spot's output and a successful initial-value check (it failed on 4 of 100 random formulas); Spot and the input and output code remain trusted. Against CASAAL, whose automata we convert with our own importer, ours have the same size on the negated requirements that both tools handle, except on three patterns where they are smaller, and TChecker stores comparable numbers of states on the 18 products that every variant completed. The optimizer does not change what TChecker explores, but in single runs the all-copy export of two bounded-response requirements did not finish in 300~s without it. Generation is slower than CASAAL's on deeply nested requirements, where Spot dominates. Of 22 seeded defects, 18 change the language and a random-word test misses five; Rocq rejects all 22 transcribed mutants, showing that the proofs are sensitive to edits, not that they detect defects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Safety and liveness requirements of real-time systems are commonly written in Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) and checked by translating the negated requirement into a timed B\"uchi automaton (TBA), which a timed model checker such as UPPAAL~\cite{[BGD04]} or TChecker~\cite{tchecker} composes with the system model. The size and shape of that automaton matter in practice. Clocks multiply the symbolic state space the checker has to explore; repeated bounds and resets that never influence a later guard enlarge the automaton without changing its language; and UPPAAL accepts only conjunctive guards and conjunctive upper-bound invariants, whereas merging transitions naturally produces disjunctions.

Optimizing a TBA is risky. A pass that is wrong in a rare case silently changes the set of timed words the property accepts, and a model checker then reports a verdict about a different requirement. The passes also interact: propagating a lower bound can make a transition contradictory. Splitting a disjunctive invariant into copies by a single entry choice is safe only if the entry condition picks a copy whose invariant admits at least as much time as every other copy; the all-copy policy of Section~\ref{sec:split} avoids that condition by entering every copy. The seeded-defect study of Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds} shows that a random-word test can miss defects of this kind.

This paper presents a post-processing pipeline for TBAs whose every pass is mechanically verified in Rocq, together with its use in a translator from MTL. The \emph{optimization} stage synthesizes upper-bound location invariants, propagates them into guards and entry bounds, removes contradictory transitions and dead resets, merges clocks that are always reset together, normalizes guards, and merges equivalent transitions and locations. The \emph{export} stage merges transitions by disjoining their guards, eliminates disjunctive invariants by creating copies with conjunctive invariants and clock-difference entry conditions, and normalizes the result so that guards and invariants are conjunctive. We prove that each pass preserves the timed-word language of every input TBA, that the optimizer does so for every number of rounds, and that the output of export has the conjunctive shape UPPAAL needs.

The passes act on TBAs, whatever their origin. To obtain an end-to-end guarantee for MTL requirements we compose them with a translation whose correctness was established separately in Rocq~\cite{faresmtl}: a verified translation of MTL$_{0,\infty}$ into a TBA, factored through clocked Linear Temporal Logic and proved correct for a verified propositional B\"uchi backend. Section~\ref{sec:composition} states the resulting theorem, which has no hypothesis on the backend when the verified backend is used; for Spot, which the tool runs, conformance to the backend contract is a hypothesis. This paper does not repeat the translation proofs; Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the definitions the passes are stated over, so that the article can be read on its own.

The passes work together and are proved as a composition: normalizing guards makes dead resets visible, removing them makes more clocks synchronous, and merging those lets transitions and locations merge, so the optimizer is iterated and its theorem holds for every number of rounds. The result is a \emph{modular, mechanized chain}: every stage except the call to Spot has a Rocq theorem stated against an interface, and the propositional backend is a parameter (Section~\ref{sec:modular}).

The verified passes are extracted to OCaml and run inside the translator \texttt{mtl2tba}, which calls Spot~\cite{spot} for the propositional automaton and writes UPPAAL XML. We ask four questions of the pipeline.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,labelindent=0pt]
  \item[RQ1] How does the whole translation compare with an independent translator, CASAAL~\cite{casaal_tool}, in the size of the automaton, in generation time and in the states a timed model checker stores?
  \item[RQ2] What does the export cost, which export policy can the checkers read, and where does optimization fail to help?
  \item[RQ3] Which Spot setting should a user choose, and what does the call to Spot cost on deeply nested requirements?
  \item[RQ4] What does verification add: which plausible defects does testing miss that the proofs reject?
\end{description}
The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item a modular chain from MTL to the exported timed automaton whose stages are stated against interfaces, so that the propositional backend (Spot, or any translator that meets the contract) can be replaced without changing the optimization and export theorems; Spot, the readers of its output, the XML printer and UPPAAL lie outside the proofs;
  \item a Rocq development of language-preserving TBA transformations (invariant synthesis and propagation, clock and location simplification, transition merging, and disjunctive-invariant elimination), with preservation theorems for every TBA of the formula-indexed clock interface and every number of optimization rounds;
  \item two verified policies for eliminating disjunctive location invariants by location copies, the maximal-delay policy (which may introduce clock-difference constraints) and the all-copy policy (which enters every copy and introduces none), each with a language-preservation proof; the all-copy export is the one that TChecker's liveness algorithms accept (Sections~\ref{sec:split} and~\ref{sec:export-policies});
  \item a verified export whose guards and invariants are conjunctive, and an end-to-end theorem for MTL requirements that composes it with a verified translation, without hypotheses on the propositional backend when the verified backend is used (the shipped tool runs Spot instead, Section~\ref{sec:composition});
  \item verified rewritings of the pass implementations that make the extracted code practical on automata of thousands of transitions, and an automatic default for Spot's level; and
  \item an evaluation of the whole translation against CASAAL in output size, generation time, TChecker stored states and UPPAAL verdicts, and a differential test of both tools against an independent MTL evaluator (122 inputs, 6{,}160{,}000 word checks, no disagreement);
  \item a seeded-defect study of 22 defects of our choosing injected in the extracted code: a random-word test misses five of the 18 that change the language, and the Rocq development rejects the corresponding mistakes, which measures the sensitivity of the proofs to edits and not defect detection; and
  \item negative results: no measurable model-checking gain from optimization, a large gap on one constructed workload whose cause we did not determine and that is not an effect of the verified passes, and slower generation than CASAAL on deeply nested requirements.
\end{itemize}

\section{Timed Automata and the Preservation Contract}
\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Timed words and TBAs}
A timed word is an infinite sequence $w=(a_i,t_i)_{i\in\Nat}$, where $a_i$ is an event from a finite alphabet $\Sigma$, timestamps satisfy $t_i\ge0$ and are nondecreasing, and time diverges: $\lim_{i\to\infty}t_i=+\infty$. For each position $i$, let $\delta_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$ be the time from event $i$ to the next event. A clock valuation maps a finite clock set $X$ to $\Real_{\ge0}$. Guards of a TBA are finite conjunctions of event literals and unary clock comparisons $x\bowtie c$ for $\bowtie\in\{<,\le,=,\ge,>\}$; transitions also carry a set of clocks to reset. The exported automaton (called a \emph{DTA} below, after its Rocq type) is a TBA whose guards may in addition contain diagonal comparisons $x-y\bowtie c$ (Section~\ref{sec:split}), and whose locations carry conjunctive upper-bound invariants. In the \emph{formula-indexed clock interface} used by the Rocq development, the clock set is the set of timed subformulas of a fixed formula, which serve only as clock names; an automaton with $k$ clocks is represented by choosing a formula with at least $k$ timed subformulas.

A TBA is a tuple $\mathcal A=(L,\ell_0,X,E,\mathit{Acc})$, where $L$ is a finite location set, $\ell_0$ is the initial location, $X$ is the clock set, $E$ is a finite set of transitions $(\ell,\alpha,g,Z,\ell')$, $\alpha$ is an event condition, $g$ is a clock guard, $Z\subseteq X$ is a reset set, and $\mathit{Acc}\subseteq L$ is the set of B\"uchi-accepting locations. The input automata of the verified optimization carry no location invariants; synthesis adds them as a separate annotation. Exported invariants are conjunctions of upper bounds.

The Rocq development interprets transitions over an extended timed word that records clock values and reset decisions. Here $v_i$ is the clock valuation at event $i$, and $Z_i$ records the clocks reset by the transition on that event. Since a reset occurs at $t_i$, the next-event valuation includes the elapsed interval $\delta_i$:
\[
v_{i+1}(x)=
\begin{cases}
\delta_i,&x\in Z_i,\\
v_i(x)+\delta_i,&x\notin Z_i.
\end{cases}
\]
A run chooses one enabled transition per position and visits accepting locations infinitely often. The accepted language existentially quantifies over clock-consistent extensions of the base timed word, including the initial clock values. This is the acceptance notion of the preservation theorems; conventional initialization at zero is a separate deployment concern, bridged by the verified check \path{init_free} (Section~\ref{sec:toolchain}) for the reading in which all clocks start at 0, as in UPPAAL; the check succeeds when no clock can be read before it is reset from the initial location.

MTL$_{0,\infty}$ is metric temporal logic with the pointwise semantics over timed words, whose timed operators carry intervals with lower bound $0$ or upper bound $\infty$; $\mathsf{msat}(w,i,f)$ states that $w$ satisfies $f$ at position $i$, and the requirement is satisfied by $w$ when $\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)$ holds. The definitions are those of~\cite{faresmtl}.

For an annotated automaton, an invariant must hold throughout the delay spent at its location. Since synthesized invariants are conjunctions of non-strict upper bounds and clocks are nondecreasing during a delay, holding at the event at the end of the delay is equivalent to holding throughout that stay. The proof formalizes this trace-model equivalence and uses it to show that the annotations and strengthening operations preserve timed words.

\subsection{What export must preserve}
\label{sec:export-contract}
The export must retain the same transition alphabet, B\"uchi marking, clock meaning, and timed-word language. It may change the number of locations, transitions, and clocks, and may introduce difference constraints in transition guards to choose among invariant copies. Such choices must not create or delete an accepted timed word. The target representation has conjunctive guards and conjunctive location invariants; a later symbolic representation groups transitions with identical source, target, and reset sets under Boolean labels.

The preservation theorems hold for every TBA of the formula-indexed clock interface, whether or not the MTL translator generated it; Spot's correctness and the soundness of the front end are outside them.

The proofs of the passes are mechanized in Rocq (Figure~\ref{fig:main-inv-unified} shows the invariant computation); the development of this paper (invariants, optimization, export and symbolic grouping) is 6,105 lines with 251 theorems and lemmas, on top of the translation of~\cite{faresmtl}. Each pass is a total function on TBAs with a preservation theorem listed in Table~\ref{tab:post-passes}; the reasoning is the same for all: a run of the output is mapped to a run of the input (or conversely) location by location, and the changed component (a guard, an invariant, a reset set, a clock or a location) is shown to be implied or equivalent at every event. In brief:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
\item \emph{Invariant synthesis} adds, at each location, the upper bound that the outgoing guards require; \emph{backward propagation} strengthens incoming guards with the target invariant (reset clocks become constants); \emph{forward propagation} adds the lower bounds implied by the entry bounds.
\item \emph{Contradictory transitions} (unsatisfiable guards or labels) and \emph{unreachable outgoing transitions} are removed; \emph{dead resets} (resets after which no path tests the clock before it is reset again) are removed, and \emph{synchronously reset clocks} are merged (every transition resets both clocks or neither, and those leaving the initial location reset the clock and do not test it).
\item \emph{Normalization} drops tautological and subsumed atoms; \emph{transition merging} fuses transitions only by subsumption and resolution, that is, when the union of two guards is again a conjunction (disjunctive unions are left to export); \emph{location merging} fuses locations that have the same outgoing behavior.
\end{itemize}
The definitions, the invariant semantics on the trace model and the proofs are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:passes}.

\subsection{Iterated optimization}
\label{sec:main-pipeline}
In right-to-left function-composition order, one optimization round is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{optimize\_step}={}&\mathsf{merge\_states}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{merge\_trans}\circ\mathsf{normalize}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{merge\_clocks}\circ\mathsf{dead\_resets}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{normalize}\circ\mathsf{unreachable}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{unsat\_labels}\circ\mathsf{contradictory}\\
 &\circ\mathsf{forward}\circ\mathsf{propagate}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The order is chosen so that the first normalization removes the tautology
$x\ge0$ that forward propagation can add after a reset, before dead-reset
analysis, which would otherwise treat the clock as live; we did not test
other orders. Each operation may enable
later simplifications, so the driver repeats the round until the TBA is
structurally unchanged or 50 rounds have run. The theorem is independent of
this stopping rule and holds for every $n$.

\begin{theorem}[Optimization]
\label{thm:optimization}
For every TBA $\mathcal A$ of the formula-indexed clock interface and every $n\in\Nat$, $\optimize(n,\mathcal A)$ accepts exactly the timed words accepted by $\mathcal A$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every constituent operation preserves acceptance, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:post-passes}. Their composition preserves acceptance, and induction on $n$ proves the statement for every number of rounds. The mechanized result is \path{optimize_accepts}.
\end{proof}

The theorem concerns the TBA component of the result; the extracted function also returns synthesized upper-bound invariants and entry bounds as annotations, which the export does not receive. The tool compares successive automata structurally and stops when they are equal or after 50 rounds. The theorem holds independently of whether iteration reaches a fixed point or uses all 50 rounds. Among the 29 completed benchmark inputs, the largest observed number of changed rounds is six (R6), so the cap was never reached; this is a measured property of these inputs, not a termination assumption in the proof. The practical 50-round cap is a driver policy, not a premise of \path{optimize_accepts}.

\section{Mechanically Verified Elimination of Disjunctive Invariants}
\label{sec:split}
Disjunctive invariants do not arise in the optimization of Section~\ref{sec:post}, whose synthesized invariants are conjunctions of upper bounds. They arise in export, where the upper bounds that the outgoing guards of a location impose form a disjunction of conjunctions (transitions that agree on source, target, label and resets are also merged by disjoining their guards). This section eliminates such disjunctions.

\subsection{A worked example: selecting a longest admissible delay}
\label{subsec:disjunctive-worked-example}
Suppose the invariant synthesized at a location $q$ is
$I_x\lor I_y$, where $I_x\equiv x\le3$ and $I_y\equiv y\le4$. From the
entry valuation $(x,y)=(0,0)$, the original disjunction allows a stay of up
to four time units: $I_y$ remains true after $I_x$ has expired. A single
copy carrying only $I_x$ would stop the run after three units and could lose
a timed word whose next event occurs at time four. The copies therefore
cannot be selected by an arbitrary disjunct.

The split creates $q_x$ with invariant $x\le3$ and $q_y$ with invariant
$y\le4$. A copy is enabled on entry only when it admits a delay at least as
long as every other copy. With the difference-constraint convention
$u-v\le c$, the entry predicates are
\[
  G_x \equiv x\le3\ \land\ x-y\le-1,
  \qquad
  G_y \equiv y\le4\ \land\ y-x\le1.
\]
At $(0,0)$, $G_y$ holds and $G_x$ does not, so the four-unit branch is
selected. At the tie valuation $(0,1)$, both predicates hold and both
copies admit the same maximal delay. Comparing residual times
$3-x$ and $4-y$ requires the clock-difference constraints; separate unary
guards cannot express their ordering.

The reset-adjustment step is visible when an incoming edge resets $x$ but
leaves $y$ unchanged. Write $y^{-}$ for the value of $y$ just before the
edge; immediately after the edge, $x=0$ and $y=y^{-}$. Substituting this
reset into the two entry predicates gives
\[
 G_x[x:=0]\equiv y\ge1,
 \qquad
 G_y[x:=0]\equiv y\le1.
\]
Thus $y^{-}>1$ admits only the $x$-copy, $y^{-}<1$ only the $y$-copy, and the
tie $y^{-}=1$ admits both. The comparison of two clocks has become a unary comparison
because one clock was reset. If the edge resets both clocks, a difference
constraint becomes a constant truth test; if it resets neither, the diagonal
comparison remains. The case in which only $y$ is reset is analogous. Together these are the four reset cases handled by the
verified guard-substitution function.

\subsection{General construction and the two split policies}
\label{subsec:split-construction}
For a disjunction $I_1\lor\cdots\lor I_r$ of upper-bound conjunctions, the construction creates one copy of the location per irredundant conjunction $U_k$, carrying $U_k$ as its invariant. The \emph{maximal-delay} policy admits a copy on entry only when its largest admissible delay is at least that of every other copy; this needs clock-difference (diagonal) entry guards, which are adjusted by the verified guard substitution for the reset cases of the example above. The \emph{all-copy} policy lets every copy be entered and relies on the covering property proved inside the construction (\path{disj_inv_cover}); it is nondeterministic and introduces no diagonal constraint. Both preserve the timed-word language (\path{split_accepts}, \path{split_all_accepts}); the full definitions, the proofs of both and a measured comparison on synthetic automata are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-subsec:split-detail}. On two synthetic examples the measured trade-off is not uniform: with two clocks all-copy uses four more edges but fewer guard atoms and no diagonal atoms; with six branches both give 68 edges and all-copy has fewer atoms; Section~\ref{sec:export-policies} compares the two policies on the benchmark. The default remains maximal delay; all-copy is separately verified and used whenever TChecker's liveness algorithms must read the automaton.


\section{Verified UPPAAL-Oriented Export}
\label{sec:export}
\subsection{Generated continuation example}
Supplement Figure~\ref{S-fig:R2-generated} follows an automaton generated by the current tool from the reset-completion boundary through optimization and export. It does not repeat the upstream MTL translation. In this instance, two optimization rounds remove nine transitions; export adds three explicit transitions while producing the required conjunctive internal shape.

The export continues by splitting disjunctive guards into conjunctive transitions, normalizing, removing copies that are never entered, and merging the initial location when an equivalent copy exists. A final symbolic pass groups transitions with the same source, target, and resets and stores their labels as Boolean conditions for drawings.

\subsection{Whole-export preservation and output shape}
\begin{theorem}[Export preservation]
\label{thm:export}
For every invariant-free TBA given to the verified export interface, the explicit export preserves its timed-word language. The resulting automaton has conjunctive transition guards and conjunctive location invariants.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The explicit construction composes verified transition merging, invariant splitting, guard splitting, normalization, removal of copies never entered, and initial-location merging; its timed-word preservation result is \path{export_accepts}. The output shape is established by \path{export_guards_conjunctive} and \path{export_invariants_conjunctive}. A separate symbolic grouping pass preserves timed-word acceptance by \path{symbolic_accepts} (with the extended-word result \path{symbolic_ext_accepts}).
\end{proof}
For reuse with another producer or checker, the theorem's output contract is
summarized in Supplement Table~\ref{S-tab:export-contract}: the language is preserved, guards and invariants are conjunctive, and a third-party parser or conversion needs its own correspondence proof.

The generated files for the R2 input (the input, the optimized automaton and the export)
are in \path{evaluation/generated_examples/R2/}, with their run
metadata. This example shows how the optimizer and the export interact; the synthetic suite
of Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} isolates pass effects on direct TBA records.


\section{End-to-End Guarantee for MTL Requirements}
\label{sec:composition}
The passes of Sections~\ref{sec:post}--\ref{sec:export} are stated over arbitrary TBAs. For MTL requirements they are used behind a translation $\mathsf{compile\_with}(A)$ that turns a propositional B\"uchi automaton $A$ for the clocked-LTL encoding $\T(f)$ of an MTL$_{0,\infty}$ formula $f$ into a TBA. Its correctness, in the form we need, is the result of~\cite{faresmtl}: if for every action-consistent propositional word $s$, $A$ accepts $s$ exactly when $s$ satisfies $\T(f)$ (the \emph{backend contract}, which any $A$ with the language of $\T(f)$ meets) and $f$ is well formed (nonnegative non-strict upper bounds, strictly positive bounds for the other timed operators), then
\[
\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)\ \leftrightarrow\ \mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(\mathsf{compile\_with}(A),w)
\]
for every timed word $w$ with nondecreasing timestamps (the words of Section~\ref{sec:model} are a subclass). The same work provides a propositional automaton $\mathsf{backend}(f,\T(f))$ for which the backend contract is proved in Rocq.

Our development imports these definitions and results, and instantiates the generic composition with that backend:
\begin{theorem}[End-to-end correctness with the verified backend]
\label{thm:composition}
For every $n\in\Nat$, every well-formed MTL$_{0,\infty}$ formula $f$ and every timed word $w$,
\[
  \begin{multlined}
  \mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)\ \leftrightarrow\\
  \mathsf{DTA\_accepts}\bigl(\export(\optimize(n,\mathsf{compile\_with}(\mathsf{backend}(f,\T(f))))),\,w\bigr).
  \end{multlined}
\]
The same holds with the all-copy exporter of Section~\ref{sec:split} in place of $\export$. Here the invariant-free TBA component of the result of $\optimize$ is the input of $\export$; the invariants and entry bounds that the extracted function also returns are not passed to it.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $C=\mathsf{compile\_with}(\mathsf{backend}(f,\T(f)))$, $O_n=\optimize(n,C)$ and $D_n=\export(O_n)$. The translation result gives $\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(C,w)$, the backend contract being discharged by the verified backend. Preservation by optimization (Theorem~\ref{thm:optimization}) gives $\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(C,w)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(O_n,w)$, and preservation by export (Theorem~\ref{thm:export}) gives $\mathsf{TBA\_accepts}(O_n,w)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{DTA\_accepts}(D_n,w)$. The Rocq statements are \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_verified} and \path{MTL_to_exported_all_correct_verified}; \texttt{Print Assumptions} lists only the standard axioms of the Rocq library (classical logic, functional extensionality, constructive definite description, and the real-number axiom of the standard library).
\end{proof}
The general form \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_with} replaces the verified backend by any automaton $A$ that satisfies the backend contract. The verified backend performs no simplification and is a proof witness only; it is not the one used by the extracted tool.

The theorem that the shipped tool instantiates is \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_recur_weak_with}. Its statement is: for every $n$, every well-formed $f$, every propositional automaton $A$ for the rewritten formula $\mathsf{recur}(f)$ that accepts the action-consistent words satisfying the weakened clocked encoding of $\mathsf{recur}(f)$, and every timed word $w$, if \path{init_free} succeeds on $\export(\optimize(n,\mathsf{compile\_with}(A)))$ then $\mathsf{msat}(w,0,f)$ holds iff that automaton accepts $w$ under the zero-initialized reading. Spot supplies $A$; whether its output satisfies the hypothesis on $A$ is not proved, and is tested only by the translation validation and the differential checks of Section~\ref{sec:verification-adds}. After a run, a user may conclude that the exported automaton accepts the requested language provided Spot's automaton meets the backend contract, \path{init_free} succeeded, and the trusted components listed in Section~\ref{sec:toolchain} behaved correctly.

No convergence premise is imposed on $n$ (Section~\ref{sec:post}). The conclusion concerns the internal exported record and the existential initial-clock semantics of Section~\ref{sec:model}. A second theorem, \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_with}, gives the zero-initialized reading whenever the check \path{init_free} succeeds on the exported automaton. Neither theorem covers the HOA/LBTT importer, the XML printer or parser, or UPPAAL itself (Section~\ref{sec:toolchain}).

\subsection{A modular chain: what can be replaced}
\label{sec:modular}
Theorem~\ref{thm:composition} is a composition of stages, and each is
stated against an interface, not against the stage before it.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Clocked-LTL translation} $\T$: from an MTL$_{0,\infty}$ formula to a
    formula over event, clock and reset atoms (proved in~\cite{faresmtl}).
  \item \emph{Propositional backend}: any B\"uchi automaton $A$ that accepts the
    action-consistent propositional words satisfying $\T(f)$, the backend
    contract of Section~\ref{sec:composition}. The theorem
    \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_with} takes the contract as a hypothesis;
    \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_verified} discharges it with the verified
    backend, which is a proof witness and slow.
  \item \emph{Reset completion} \path{compile_with}: from $A$ to a TBA of the
    formula-indexed clock interface (proved in~\cite{faresmtl}).
  \item \emph{Optimization} and \emph{export}: language-preserving passes over
    every TBA of that interface (this paper).
\end{enumerate}
Two consequences follow, and the development already uses the first.

First, the backend is a parameter. Any LTL-to-B\"uchi translator whose output
satisfies the contract can replace Spot without touching a proof: the tool
reads the output of any translator that accepts Spot's command line
(\texttt{-spot}) or whose output is converted to HOA or LBTT, and the
guarantee of the chain is then that of the contract the user, or a proof,
establishes for that translator. In this paper we changed Spot's level, its options and the format in which we read its output, and we replaced the list scans of the passes with tables with equality lemmas (Section~\ref{sec:speedups}), each time with no change to the statement of any theorem. The verified
backend of the development shows the other end of the range: a translator
with a proof of the contract and a poor running time.

Second, the stages in front of the optimizer can be replaced together. A construction that produces a TBA directly from the MTL formula, as CASAAL does with the construction of Li et al.~\cite{li_spin7}, would be composed with the same optimization and export theorems; the first equivalence of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:composition} would be replaced by the correctness theorem of the new construction. We have not written such a construction; it would remove the dependency on Spot.

This design has a price, which Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures: on deeply nested requirements the generic LTL translator is the slow part of the chain, at 60 to 90\% of CASAAL's whole translation time on seven levels in the one profile we made (Section~\ref{sec:casaal-time}), while the verified stages are not. A purpose-built front end might reduce that time; this is an expectation based on one profile, not a measurement.

\section{Tool Architecture and Use in UPPAAL}
\label{sec:toolchain}
\subsection{Implementation and proof boundary}
The OCaml module \texttt{optim.ml} is extracted from Rocq and contains the
verified optimization and export functions. The integrated executable also
contains handwritten input, iteration, and output code. These adapters and
the XML/DOT printers are outside the transformation proofs. A converter from
an external automaton format must establish its own correspondence with the
Rocq TBA type. A clean Rocq 9.0.1 build, independent \texttt{coqchk} run,
and OCaml 5.4.0 rebuild are recorded in the accompanying artifact audit.
Rerunning the UPPAAL 5.0.0 queries reproduces the recorded verdicts.

The project-wide axiom \path{LTL_TO_BUCHI_CORRECT} states the assumed correctness of the upstream LTL-to-B\"uchi backend and is used only by convenience theorems. The theorems \path{optimize_accepts}, \path{split_accepts}, \path{export_accepts} and \path{MTL_to_exported_correct_with} do not use it: \texttt{Print Assumptions} lists only standard classical Rocq library principles (including \path{ClassicalDedekindReals.sig_forall_dec}), and the artifact records the results.

In the integrated workflow the translation of~\cite{faresmtl} produces the TBA at the optimizer boundary; this paper concerns only the subsequent optimizer and exporter. The driver then applies export to the optimized automaton.

\subsection{Choosing the Spot level: automatic mode and manual override}
\label{sec:spot-level}
The propositional automaton is produced by \texttt{ltl2tgba -B --<level> [-x <options>] --hoa} and read back by the tool (the reader is outside the proofs). The theorems hold for every automaton that meets the backend contract, so level and options affect running time strongly, the size of the result little, and correctness not at all. No level is best everywhere: level \texttt{low} is fast on conjunctions of bounded responses and slow on deeply nested bounded eventualities, and level \texttt{any} the reverse; Spot's simulation-based reduction removed nothing on our inputs and takes more than half of Spot's time at level \texttt{any}, so we switch it off (\texttt{-x ba-simul=0}). The default is therefore an \emph{automatic mode} (\texttt{-spot-level auto}): it runs Spot at level \texttt{low} and, if Spot has not finished after a budget of $b$ seconds (\texttt{-spot-budget}, default 5), stops it and runs level \texttt{any} with \texttt{ba-simul=0} on the formula simplified by the proved simplification \path{ltl_simp_correct}. Both steps are instances of the verified translation and only Spot remains trusted. The fallback costs at most $b$ seconds, and the choice depends on a wall-clock measurement, so the level, and with it the run time, can differ between runs and machines; the mode can be overridden with \texttt{-spot-level}, \texttt{-simp} and \texttt{-spot-x}, and benchmarking should fix the level. The measurements behind these statements are in Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:spot-level-detail}; Section~\ref{sec:casaal-time} reports automatic and manual times side by side.

\subsection{Verified speedups, input contract and artifact layout}
\label{sec:speedups}
The passes were first written for the proofs, with list scans that dominated the time on the larger automata. We replaced them by per-location tables, a cheaper merge test, a duplicate-free initial-value search and a single computation of the formula's clock list. Each replacement carries a proof of equality with the definition it replaces, so every theorem applies to the Rocq definitions from which the fast code is extracted. The generated automata are byte-identical to those of the previous implementation on 92 runs (all benchmark patterns except N6--N8, R7, R8 and the largest positive forms, in both polarities; a regression test, not a proof). The effect is as follows. On the seven-level negated nested requirement N7 (129 locations and 10{,}930 transitions before optimization, \texttt{-simp -spot-level any}, Spot's own time not included), reset completion falls from 6.1 to 0.7~s, optimization from 15.2 to 2.2~s and the initial-value check from 15.8 to 1.1~s; on the positive form of six conjoined bounded responses (R6), optimization falls from 19.6 to 0.29~s and the default export from 9.6 to 0.08~s. These are single runs of single stages, as printed by the statistics line of the tool, not the wall-clock times of complete runs reported in Section~\ref{sec:export-policies}.
Two further changes concern the call to Spot and are outside the proofs: Spot's simulation-based reduction is switched off at level \texttt{any}, which cut Spot's time on the nested requirements by a factor of up to 2.8 (the best case, in single runs) without changing the automaton on our benchmark, and the tool reads Spot's HOA output instead of LBTT (identical tool messages on 184 runs; XML and DOT files identical on 144 and, on the others, differing only in the order of conjuncts or labels). An external automaton enters the verified stages through the input contract (location acceptance, formula-indexed clocks, no location invariants), and \path{tba_opt} reads such an automaton from a text file. The list of rewritings, the input contract and the artifact layout are in Supplement Sections~\ref{S-sec:speedups-detail}, \ref{S-sec:generic-input-detail} and \ref{S-sec:layout}.

\subsection{Initialization interface}
The preservation theorems use existential initial clock values, whereas UPPAAL initializes clocks to zero. The proved \path{MTL_to_exported_correct0_with} theorem gives the zero-initialized result for the formula-indexed workflow when the verified \path{init_free} check succeeds. The check succeeds on F1--F14, R1--R6 and N1--N5 in both polarities and on the three case-study requirements, fails on 4 of the 100 random formulas (the tool then warns that the zero-initialized result is not covered by the theorem), and applies equally to an external automaton.

\subsection{Using exported automata in UPPAAL}
The language-preservation theorem concerns infinite timed words with one event at each position, nondecreasing timestamps, divergent time, and the development's clock initialization semantics. A UPPAAL composition must preserve the assumptions needed to interpret its traces in that model:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
  \item \emph{Events and time.} The system must synchronize each observable event with the property automaton, prevent zero-delay consecutive events (for example, reset a system clock $z$ on each emission and require $z>0$ on the next), and admit time-divergent executions; reachability alone does not rule out a Zeno continuation.
  \item \emph{Initial clocks.} UPPAAL initializes clocks to zero, so the zero-initialized conclusion requires the \path{init_free} check to succeed on the exported automaton.
  \item \emph{Acceptance.} UPPAAL's reachability interface does not check B\"uchi acceptance. For safety, use a violation automaton with an accepting sink that is reached when a violation occurs and loops on every event; liveness requires a B\"uchi emptiness checker restricted to time-divergent runs, for which the artifact uses TChecker.
  \item \emph{Time constraints in products.} Location invariants can block time in a product and thereby remove system behaviors, so an exported property automaton is not a passive observer unless its effect is separately justified; check deadlock or timelock conditions on the system.
  \item \emph{Numbers.} The tool accepts integer bounds in $[0,2^{30}]$, while the Rocq theorems are over real-valued clocks; the extracted arithmetic and the UPPAAL printer are covered only on that restricted domain.
\end{enumerate}

The generated XML has a \texttt{Property} template for the automaton and an \texttt{Env} template for standalone exploration; in a system check the system replaces \texttt{Env} and synchronizes its observable events with \texttt{Property}, and the event \texttt{other} denotes any event outside the alphabet of the formula. Accepting locations are named \texttt{A}$n$; the name does not make UPPAAL check B\"uchi acceptance. For a safety requirement, under (C1)--(C3) the query \texttt{E<> P.A} on a violation automaton composed with such a system detects a violating behavior; this query-level argument is not proved by the Rocq theorems.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\emph{Internal validity.} The theorems concern the Rocq model of automata; the extracted OCaml functions are the verified ones, but the surrounding tool is not. The trusted base is: Spot and the reader of its output; the realization of reals by floating point and the extraction mechanism; the parser, the iteration driver, the pretty printers and the UPPAAL XML writer; the importer used for CASAAL's automata; Graphviz; the OCaml and Rocq toolchains; UPPAAL and TChecker; the check \path{init_free}; and the project axiom \path{LTL_TO_BUCHI_CORRECT}, which only convenience theorems use. We reduce this risk with a second printer, a reader check (\texttt{-check}) and independent exploration of the case-study models, but these are tests, not proofs.

\emph{Construct validity.} CASAAL implements the semantics of Li et al.\ with integer bounds and mutually exclusive events, and it reports transition-based acceptance. We compare only configurations whose CASAAL encoding is exact in that setting and convert its acceptance to locations; the importer and the accepting-state convention are our choices, and a different encoding could give different counts. Stored states are a property of the pair (automaton, checker), and generation times compare a Windows executable with our tool under WSL~2, so they are not precise ratios; differences below about 0.3~s are of the order of the process start. CASAAL is the only comparator: MightyL was not run (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\emph{External validity.} The requirement patterns are a small corpus drawn from the literature and from parameterized families, and the system models are small. Results do not predict behavior on industrial specifications, and we make no speedup claim from optimization.

\emph{Conclusion validity.} Every number in the tables is produced by scripts in the archive from raw tool output; the comparisons are counts over a fixed list of configurations, with no significance tests. Times are medians of three to five runs without spread, and the export-scalability and N8 results are single runs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a Rocq-verified optimization and export pipeline for timed B\"uchi automata, its composition with a verified MTL translation into a modular, mechanized chain whose stages are stated against interfaces, and an evaluation of the whole chain against the independent translator CASAAL. Each pass preserves the timed-word language of every input TBA, the optimizer does so for every number of rounds, and the output of export has the conjunctive guards and invariants UPPAAL requires.

On the negated requirements that a model checker receives, our automata have the same size as CASAAL's, after our own conversion of its acceptance, wherever both tools finish, except on three small patterns (F5, F6, F8) where ours are smaller; the automata are isomorphic on only 11 of 27 patterns. Over the 18 products on which every variant completed, TChecker stores 204{,}564 states on ours and 204{,}819 on CASAAL's, and R3 alone supplies 168{,}484 of each; the optimizer changes none of the counts. On the positive form of the bounded responses, composed with a constructed system that violates the requirement and exercises every response, CASAAL's counts grow by factors of 19, 53 and 37 on three consecutive responses (1{,}736{,}096 states on R4 against 114 for ours). The cause is not determined: the gap is present in the zone graph of the converted CASAAL automaton, it shrinks from 13--15{,}000 to 2.8--14 times under inclusion subsumption, and it is not removed or caused by the verified passes (Section~\ref{sec:casaal-stress}). The optimization passes shrink the automaton (the seven-level nested automaton falls from 10{,}930 to 5{,}105 transitions, the size of CASAAL's but not an isomorphic automaton) without changing what the checkers explore; in single runs they decide whether the all-copy export of R5 and R6 finishes within 300~s. Generation is slower than CASAAL's on nested requirements; in the one profile we made (N7), Spot alone took 60 to 90\% of CASAAL's whole translation time.

What the verified chain adds is assurance over all inputs, which a random-word test can miss: of 22 defects of our choosing in the extracted code, 18 change the language, a random-word test misses five of them, and the corresponding mistakes in the Rocq definitions are all rejected within seconds at named lemmas. Since the transcriptions are ours and the test is a weak baseline, this shows the sensitivity of the proofs to edits, not defect detection. It is evidence about our own pipeline and says nothing against CASAAL.

The chain is built to be changed at its front: a direct MTL-to-TBA construction in the style of CASAAL could replace the whole front end under the same theorems; whether it would close the gap on nested requirements is untested. Future work includes such a verified construction, which would also close the trust gap on Spot, and evaluation on industrial TBA corpora.
