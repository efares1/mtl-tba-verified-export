\subsection{Certificate-checked passes (RQ5)}
\label{sec:eval-checked}
\emph{Pruning on the translator's own automata.} We ran \texttt{mtl2tba -prune} (without splitting) on the 29 benchmark configurations and the 100 random formulas, in both polarities (\path{evaluation/prune_survey.tsv}); the checker accepted every certificate. On the requirements as written, the explicit transitions of the exported automaton fall from 1{,}504 to 1{,}025 on the benchmark (15 of 29 automata reduced; R6 640 to 448) and from 2{,}384 to 1{,}979 on the random formulas (21 of 100 reduced, none increased). On the negated requirements the effect is small (913 to 912 and 1{,}203 to 1{,}142). TChecker stores the same states with and without pruning on the reference products of R2--R4 (70, 92, 114): like the optimizer, pruning shrinks our own automata without changing what the checker explores.

\emph{Pruning on CASAAL's automaton.} Table~\ref{tab:stress} adds two columns to the workload of Section~\ref{sec:casaal-stress}: CASAAL's automaton after pruning, and after the verified splitting of transitions by cells of letters followed by pruning. Pruning alone removes a part of the extra states (890 to 781 on R2, 47{,}008 to 34{,}483 on R3, 1{,}736{,}096 to 1{,}202{,}691 on R4). Splitting first, so that overlapping labels can be compared, brings the count to exactly ours on R2--R5 (70, 92, 114, 136), and R5, which did not finish for CASAAL's automaton, finishes in 1.5~ms. The ordered clocks of the checker are what makes this possible: with a certifier restricted to equality on every clock read by a guard (\path{-prune-eq}; the checker is unchanged and accepts the certificate), splitting and pruning remove nothing and TChecker stores 890, 47{,}008 and 1{,}736{,}096 states on R2--R4, as without pruning (R5 was not run). On this workload the extra states of CASAAL's automaton are therefore explored through transitions that other transitions dominate and that the checked pass removes. This does not come from our conversion of CASAAL's acceptance (Section~\ref{sec:casaal-size}): for R2--R5 CASAAL prints no acceptance mark, so the importer keeps its $2^n$ locations and marks every state accepting, and the transitions that TChecker receives are those that its printed labels denote, 12 to 13\% of which repeat another transition of the same edge and the others of which differ in target, guard or resets (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:casaal-label-detail}). What is ours is the reading of the labels as mutually exclusive letters and their expansion into one transition per disjunct.

\emph{Pruning on independently generated TBAs.} We ran both variants on the 600 automata of Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}: every certificate was accepted and the bounded word test finds no language difference. Pruning is not generally strong: it removes 24 of 3{,}054 transitions on 23 of the 300 graphs and nothing on the 300 monitors, and splitting enlarges the automata (3{,}054 to 5{,}316 and 1{,}930 to 3{,}078). It pays where a translation guesses which obligations are pending, as on CASAAL's automata.

\emph{Clock reuse.} The pass removes 47 of the 201 clocks of the optimized negated automata (10 of the 29 benchmark automata and 16 of the 100 random formulas are reduced; 15 of 205 clocks of the forms as written), the checker accepts every certificate, and the numbers of locations and transitions never change since the pass only renames (\path{evaluation/alloc_survey.tsv}). To test the checker, we sent the clocks of 69 negated automata to random clocks (552 allocations; \path{evaluation/alloc_fuzz.tsv}): it accepted 343 and rejected 209, and applied without the check, 112 of the rejected allocations changed the language on 200 random words against an independent evaluator, and none of the accepted ones did. For the model checker, we composed the negated automata of the bounded-response families B$(n,k)$ ($n$ responses with bound $k$, with $n$ timers that reuse one clock) and of three benchmark patterns with a system that satisfies the requirement, and ran TChecker on 15 pairs, five interleaved runs each, pinned to one core. Verdicts and stored states are identical in every pair. On the nine products with at least 8{,}000 stored states, TChecker is 17\% to 28\% faster with reuse (median) and uses up to 32\% less memory on the largest; on the smallest the difference is within noise. On the benchmark requirements whose negated automaton loses a clock and whose product TChecker completes (F11--F13, R2, R3), the ratio is 0.81 to 0.90 with equal states (Supplement Section~\ref{S-sec:alloc-natural}). The families are constructed, one machine and one checker were used, and UPPAAL was not measured.
